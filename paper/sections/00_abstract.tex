The construction of stability charts of linear autonomous time-delay systems is revisited in the frequency domain. The number of unstable characteristic roots is obtained from the argument principle by tracking the phase of the characteristic function along the imaginary axis; the winding integral is reformulated as an ordinary differential equation and solved by an adaptive high-order integrator, with the integrand evaluated exactly by forward-mode automatic differentiation. The adaptive frequency march makes practically unbounded integration ranges affordable, and the result is self-validating: the distance of the computed winding number from the nearest integer is a dimensionless error measure that requires no problem-specific tuning and that scales with the requested tolerance, so the accuracy of the count can be prescribed in advance; the one failure mode this residual cannot see -- a root peak skipped immediately at a stability boundary -- is exposed by a built-in cross-check against the independently tracked root. During the same sweep, the characteristic root closest to the integration line -- typically the rightmost root -- is estimated as a computational by-product and is subsequently refined by higher-order corrections built on the same automatic derivatives. This root estimate turns the otherwise piecewise-constant root-counting map into an interpolable field, so a single chart simultaneously displays the number of unstable roots and the spectral gap of the stable domain. The true stability boundary is traced at high resolution by the multi-dimensional bisection method driven by the estimated root distance as a scalar objective, and the most robust parameter point is located as the center of the largest circle inscribed in the stable domain. The characteristic function is extracted algorithmically from a simulation-ready right-hand side, including singular mass matrices (delay differential--algebraic systems). Retarded, neutral, distributed-delay, transcendental, high-dimensional and fractional-order case studies demonstrate accurate chart generation at interactive rates (complete charts in fractions of a second on a desktop machine, after a one-time startup cost), and comparisons with adaptive quadrature and semi-discretization quantify the efficiency of the approach. The method is available in the open-source Julia package \pkg{InterpolatedNyquist.jl}.
