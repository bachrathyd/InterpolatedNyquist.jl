\section{Parallel execution: from one core to a graphics processor}\label{sec:parallel}

The absolute chart times quoted so far depend on the hardware they were
measured on, and a reader with a different machine cannot transfer them. A
more useful description separates two numbers: the cost of one parameter point
on one thread, and how that cost divides among many threads. This section
measures both, on CPUs and GPUs, and shows that the second is almost ideal --
which is what makes real-time charts possible, and what lets anyone predict the
chart time on their own hardware.

\subsection{Why the sweep parallelizes perfectly}\label{sec:parallel-why}

A stability chart is an embarrassingly parallel computation: the parameter
points are independent, each needs only the user's characteristic function and
the march of Section~\ref{sec:unwrap}, and nothing is exchanged between them.
The discrete march adds three properties that a parallel machine rewards: its
state is a fixed handful of scalars (no allocation, no solver object), every
step is the same short sequence of operations (one evaluation of $\Dfun$ and
$\Dfun'$ by dual numbers, a comparison, a step update), and its cost per point
varies only moderately across a chart -- the median point of the three
benchmark systems needs $31$, $52$ and $129$ evaluations
(Table~\ref{tab:unwrap}), and the maximum is a small multiple of the median. The
same Julia source therefore runs as a multithreaded CPU loop and as a GPU kernel
with one thread per point; we implemented it once with
\pkg{KernelAbstractions.jl}, which compiles it for both.

Figure~\ref{fig:parallel} shows the time of a chart of $10^{6}$ points as a
function of the number of threads, and Table~\ref{tab:parallel} extracts the
characteristic numbers. On the CPU the result is what the independence of the
points promises. One thread needs $\ParCpuOneUs\,\mu$s per point; every
doubling of the threads halves the time, so that $16$ threads are
$\ParCpuSixteenSpeedup$ times faster and $48$ threads $\ParCpuFortyEightSpeedup$
times -- the small deficit coming from hyper-threading, where two threads share
one core. The six-core desktop follows the same line up to its six cores
($5.6$ times) and gains another factor of $1.6$ from its twelve hardware
threads. A chart that takes $3.8$\,s on one core ($10^{6}$ points of the
fourth-order benchmark) thus takes $0.1$\,s on a $48$-thread server.

On the GPU the same picture holds, shifted by a constant factor. A single GPU
thread is slower than a CPU thread -- $\ParGpuOneUsLo$--$\ParGpuOneUsHi\,\mu$s
per point in Float32, five to eight times the CPU time, because a GPU core runs
at a lower clock and executes one instruction at a time without the
speculation and large caches of a CPU core. From there the time again falls in
proportion to the number of threads, over four decades, until the device is
saturated. Two short deviations from the ideal line are instructive. Below
$32$ threads the gain is sublinear, because a GPU executes threads in groups of
$32$ (a \emph{warp}) in lockstep: the threads of one warp advance together, and
a warp is only as fast as its slowest point. Between $64$ and $256$ threads the
curve flattens, because a single thread block runs on one multiprocessor; the
ideal slope resumes as soon as the blocks spread over the device. Beyond some
$10^{4}$--$10^{5}$ threads every multiprocessor is busy and the curves level
off at the saturated cost of each card.

\subsection{A performance model for any hardware}\label{sec:parallel-model}

The measurements condense into a model with two hardware numbers. If one
thread needs $t_1$ per point and the device sustains $P_{\mathrm{eff}}$
concurrent marches, a chart of $N$ points on $n$ threads takes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parmodel}
  T(N,n) \approx \frac{N\,t_1}{\min\{n,\,P_{\mathrm{eff}}\}} .
\end{equation}
On a CPU, $P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the number of cores (plus a fraction for
hardware threads). On the four GPUs it is $1.2$ to $2.6$ times the number of
CUDA cores in Float32 (Table~\ref{tab:parallel}): a GPU core can keep more than
one march in flight because it switches between warps while one waits for
memory or for the result of a long instruction. Since $t_1$ in Float32 differs
by less than a factor of two between the four cards, the saturated chart time
is set almost entirely by the number of cores -- the chart is
compute-bound, not memory-bound, which is expected for a method whose state fits
in registers. The model thus predicts the time on an unlisted card from its core
count and the per-point cost measured once on any GPU, within a factor of
about two; clock rate, register pressure (which limits the number of resident
warps) and the per-point variation of the step count account for the
remainder. The per-point cost itself scales with the cost of one evaluation of
$\Dfun$ and with the number of evaluations: the two-mode turning model, with
four times the evaluations of the fourth-order benchmark, saturates the
RTX PRO 6000 at $3.0$\,ns instead of $\ParBestNsPerPoint$\,ns per point.

Float64 follows the same model with a larger $t_1$, and Fig.~\ref{fig:parallel}
shows the consequence of the hardware design. Graphics-oriented cards (T4, L4,
RTX PRO 6000) execute double-precision operations at $1/32$--$1/64$ of the
single-precision rate, so their saturated Float64 time is $23$--$41$ times the
Float32 time, whereas the A100, built for scientific computing, pays a factor of
three. Since the Float32 chart equals the Float64 chart except at a single
flagged point of two million (Table~\ref{tab:gpu}), Float64 serves as a
reference on the GPU rather than as a production format.

The horizontal lines of Fig.~\ref{fig:parallel} translate the per-point cost
into frame rates. A chart refreshed thirty times per second leaves
$33$\,ms per frame; for a full-HD chart ($2.07\cdot10^{6}$ points) this needs
$16$\,ns per point, and for an 8K chart ($7680\times4320\approx3.3\cdot10^{7}$
points) $1$\,ns per point. A single CPU core is more than two orders of
magnitude above the first line, and even the $48$-thread server is a factor of
six above it (a full-HD chart in $0.2$\,s, i.e.\ five frames per second, or
real time at a quarter of the resolution). Every one of the four GPUs is
below it, and the fastest card, at $\ParBestNsPerPoint$\,ns per point --
$\ParBestVsCpu$ times the throughput of one CPU core, an effective parallelism of
$\ParBestPeff$ -- is below the 8K line as well: a full-HD chart takes
$\ParBestFullHDms$\,ms and an 8K chart $\ParBestEightKms$\,ms. The chart can
therefore be recomputed \emph{while a slider is being moved}: the delay, a gain
or a damping ratio is changed, and the complete chart -- here more than thirty
million stability decisions with the rightmost root of each -- follows the
motion of the hand without visible delay. Table~\ref{tab:gpu} gives the
measured timings with the production settings. With one thread per point and
no thread limit -- the schedule of production runs, somewhat faster than the
work queue used to control the thread count in Fig.~\ref{fig:parallel} -- the
\GpuBest{} computes the full-HD chart in $\GpuBestFthirtytwo$\,ms and the 8K
chart in $\GpuEightKFthirtytwo$\,ms in Float32; a slider loop that changes the
delay for every frame and copies the complete result back to the host runs at
$\GpuBestSlider$\,ms per frame in full HD. In an interactive application only
the coloured display image needs to leave the device, which adds a few
milliseconds per frame (\code{gpu/results/interactive\_G4.csv}).

It must be stressed that these frame rates belong to the given problem. The
cost per point is proportional to the cost of one evaluation of $\Dfun$ and to
the number of evaluations, so a model whose $\Dfun$ is a large determinant or
whose phase ripple decays slowly (Table~\ref{tab:evals}) moves all curves of
Fig.~\ref{fig:parallel} upwards by the corresponding factor -- the two-mode
turning model, for instance, runs at about ten frames per second at 8K and
above $150$ frames per second at full HD on the same card. What does not
change is the slope: whatever the characteristic function, the chart time
divides by the number of threads, from a single core to tens of thousands of
GPU cores.

\paragraph{Narrow number formats.} Since the march needs only the
\emph{phase} of $\Dfun$ to a fraction of a radian, the characteristic function
can even be evaluated in half precision (Table~\ref{tab:precision}). Float16
is accurate wherever no root is close to the line: after rescaling the
frequency into its range it yields $\GpuFsixteenWrong\,\%$ wrong counts that
are \emph{all} flagged, and $\GpuFsixteenFlagged\,\%$ flagged points in total;
re-checking the flagged points in Float32 -- in place on the device: gather,
march, scatter -- restores the exact chart. It does not, however, make the
chart faster: with the kernel compiled as a single inlined loop, the Float16
evaluation takes $\GpuBestFsixteen$\,ms against $\GpuBestFthirtytwoFifteen$\,ms in
Float32 on the \GpuBest{} (Table~\ref{tab:gpu}), because the march is limited by
the number of instructions issued, not by the width of the arithmetic, and the
conversions between the formats cost as much as the narrower operations save.
(An earlier version of the kernel, which passed the dual numbers through local
memory, did run several times faster in Float16 -- the gain came from the
smaller memory traffic, and disappeared once that traffic was removed.) Float32
is therefore the production format on every card measured, and the value of the
narrow formats lies in what they reveal about the method: how little precision
the phase needs. BFloat16, with only seven mantissa
bits, flags $14\,\%$ of the chart, and the 8-bit and 4-bit formats are useless
for this purpose: with two or three mantissa bits even the \emph{parameters} of
a chart are quantized to a handful of distinct values per octave, so that
$95$--$100\,\%$ of the points are flagged and up to one in ten counts is
wrong; Float4 gets every count wrong. Load balancing, finally, turned out to be
unnecessary: a dynamic work queue, in which threads that finish early fetch new
points, was not faster than one thread per point on any card, because the cost
per point varies only moderately and the hardware scheduler absorbs the
difference.
