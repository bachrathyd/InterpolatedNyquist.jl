\section{Performance analysis}\label{sec:performance}

\subsection{Convergence and efficiency of the integrators}\label{sec:performance-convergence}

\end{figure}

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \caption{Count error $|\Zraw-\Zint|$ (left block) and single-point CPU time
  (right block) of the back-ends at selected tolerances; system and point as

  \maybeinput{tables/tab_convergence.tex}
\end{table}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} and Table~\ref{tab:convergence} quantify the
choice of back-end, and three points deserve emphasis. First, all adaptive
back-ends track the requested tolerance over the studied range -- with the
error settling roughly two orders of magnitude above it, the rule of thumb
$\varepsilon\approx100\,\mathrm{tol}$ of Section~\ref{sec:integerness} --
until they meet a common floor of $\approx8\cdot10^{-7}$ set by the tail
truncated at $\wmax=10^{6}$ and the estimated leading order, not by any
integrator. Second, the order of the pair matters far more than its family.
Across the loose half of the ladder the classical fifth-order
pair~\cite{tsitouras2011runge} under-resolves the $\omega^{-1}$ ripple of
this velocity-feedback system, leaving count errors of $0.2$--$0.34$ --
within a hair of the rounding threshold -- and even at the package default
$10^{-5}$ it leaves $9\cdot10^{-2}$ where the ninth-order
default~\cite{verner2010numerically} leaves $6\cdot10^{-5}$: three orders
of magnitude, for a factor of $2.4$ in time. The linearly implicit
Rosenbrock
pair~\cite{rosenbrock1963some,shampine1997matlab,hairer1996solving} is
uniformly dominated, as
expected once one recognizes that the problem has no stiffness for it to
exploit (Section~\ref{sec:phaseode}); at tight tolerances it needs seconds
for a single parameter point where the ninth-order pair needs a quarter of
one, which is why the sweep in Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence} stops each method
once a point exceeds the cost cap. Third, at the default setting every
back-end still rounds to the correct count, so the choice of integrator
does not change the chart -- what differs, by orders of magnitude, is the
\emph{margin} to the rounding threshold, and the default is chosen for that
margin rather than for raw speed.

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \caption{Approaching a Hopf boundary of the showcase system at distance
  $\Delta P$ in the gain. The peak half-width equals the true $|\sest|$,
  proportional to $\Delta P$; the tracked estimate reproduces it until it
  saturates at the solver-tolerance floor (last two rows). The marching
  solver silently loses one root once the peak falls below its step
  resolution, while the integer residual stays small
  ($\Zraw$ shifts by exactly one).}\label{tab:boundarystress}
  \maybeinput{tables/tab_boundary_stress.tex}
\end{table}

The one situation in which the back-ends genuinely differ is the immediate
vicinity of a stability boundary (Table~\ref{tab:boundarystress}). As the
crossing root approaches the integration line, the integrand peak narrows
in proportion to $\Delta P$ (measured half-width $6.6\cdot10^{-8}$ at
$\Delta P=10^{-6}$). By $\Delta P=10^{-8}$ the marching solver steps clean
over the peak, loses exactly $\pm\pi$, reports one root too few -- and
returns a success flag and a perfectly healthy integer residual.
Gauss--Kronrod survives the same test, though not by cleverness: the
persistent delay ripple forces it to subdivide finely across the whole
range anyway, and at the tight tolerance of this stress test ($10^{-8}$)
that refinement still resolves the $\Delta P=10^{-10}$ peak. At the package
default the margin is thinner: there the same experiment
(\code{data/peak\_repair.csv}) shows the quadrature losing the count
silently at $\Delta P=10^{-10}$, while the repaired march of
Section~\ref{sec:showcase-tolerance} -- steered onto the peak by the
$|\Dfun|^{2}$ minimum rather than searching for it -- still counts
correctly there.

The practical consequences are mild. Landing within $\sim10^{-8}$ of the
boundary is a measure-zero event that a chart grid hits, if ever, in a
single pixel, and the damage is a count off by one, not a wild outlier. The
MDBM trace is insensitive to it: its objective tends to zero at the
boundary regardless of the sign, so a miscount displaces the interpolated
boundary point by no more than the skipped strip's width, $\sim10^{-8}$ in
the gain -- far below plotting resolution. And the stable/unstable
classification is affected only where the skip flips the count to or from
zero -- exactly the situation in which the tracked root (still positive,
$\sest=+4.3\cdot10^{-7}$ at $\Delta P=10^{-8}$, correctly placing a root
right of the axis) disagrees with $\Zint=0$ and the sign
test~\eqref{eq:crosscheck} fires. Since that test is available only from
the ordered march, the march remains the default.

Two operating points matter in practice, and Table~\ref{tab:presets}
separates them. With the \emph{accurate} preset -- the standard chart
window $\wmax=10^{4}$ and the default $\mathrm{tol}=10^{-5}$ -- a complete
$100\times100$ chart of a scalar characteristic function costs a fraction
of a second, and every one of the ten thousand counts matches a tight
reference. A \emph{fast} preset ($\mathrm{tol}=10^{-3}$) buys roughly a
factor of two at the price of a few dozen boundary-adjacent miscounts --
acceptable for a first look, and flagged by the diagnostics of
Section~\ref{sec:showcase-tolerance}. Beyond the presets, two user choices
dominate the cost on the showcase DDAE, spanning three orders of magnitude
together: $\wmax$ (the velocity feedback's $\omega^{-1}$ ripple makes
$\wmax=10^{6}$ cost some sixty times $\wmax=10^{4}$, while buying nothing
-- the truncated tail is five orders below the rounding threshold) and the
form of $\Dfun$ (the automatic extraction costs a factor of $\ExtractRatio$
per evaluation over the hand-derived expression,
Table~\ref{tab:extraction}). Choosing badly on both turns the $1.98$\,s
chart of Table~\ref{tab:presets} into a fifty-minute one; this is what the
asymmetric guidance of Section~\ref{sec:phaseode} means in practice.

Table~\ref{tab:gridtimings} reports the cost of a \emph{complete}
$100\times100$ chart -- ten thousand parameter points -- on a
\CPUmodel{} (\NumThreads{} threads, \RAMgb\,GB RAM, Julia~\JuliaVersion,
package revision \PkgCommit), with the per-point cost listed alongside so
that other resolutions can be scaled directly. For scalar characteristic
functions a full chart takes tens to hundreds of milliseconds, and the
entire hybrid workflow (colored sweep plus MDBM boundary) finishes well
inside one second -- genuinely interactive. The last column is worth a
glance: the fixed-step back-end is the fastest entry for the delayed systems
and gets thousands of the ten thousand counts wrong, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:alternatives}. The showcase DDAE is listed with its
hand-derived characteristic function, as are all figures in this paper; what
the automatic extraction of Section~\ref{sec:extraction} would cost instead
is measured separately in Table~\ref{tab:extraction}. Memory stays modest throughout
because the phase ODE carries a single scalar state and the sweep stores
only the five result fields; no large matrices are formed at any point.
Cold start is a one-time cost dominated by package loading: in a fresh Julia
process, loading takes $5$--$8$\,s and the first thousand-point sweep
$0.07$--$0.2$\,s thanks to the precompilation workload shipped with the
package, after which every further chart runs at the warm speeds of
Table~\ref{tab:gridtimings}.

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \caption{What the MDBM boundary trace buys: from an initial mesh with the
  listed refinement levels, the traced boundary has the equivalent resolution
  of a uniform grid of the given size, at the listed number of function
  evaluations. The saving factor is the ratio of the two point
  counts.}\label{tab:mdbm}
  \maybeinput{tables/tab_mdbm.tex}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:mdbm} makes the boundary-tracing argument of
Section~\ref{sec:mdbm} concrete. Four refinement levels from a
$20\times20$ mesh yield a boundary resolved as finely as a
$320\times320$ uniform grid would resolve it, while evaluating only the
cells that actually bracket the boundary -- a saving of $34$ to $53$ times in
function evaluations, which is precisely why the high-resolution boundary is
affordable on top of a deliberately coarse colored background. These numbers substantiate the practical claim
of the paper: the stability chart of a nontrivial delayed system refreshes
fast enough to be explored \emph{interactively}, which changes how such
charts are used in design -- parameter studies and optimization loops can
call the entire chart pipeline as an inner function.

\subsection{The discrete march: two orders of magnitude on the CPU}\label{sec:performance-unwrap}

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering

  the integration back-ends and with the discrete phase-unwrapping march of
  Section~\ref{sec:unwrap}, all measured in one session on one machine
  (\UwCPU, \UwThreads{} threads), so that the speed-up factors are free of
  hardware differences. The integration back-ends use the chart window
  $\wmax=10^{4}$, the discrete march $\wmax=10^{5}$; the wrong-$\Zint$ column
  counts the deviations from a tight-tolerance ($10^{-8}$) integration
  reference of the same chart. The turning model enters the discrete march
  with its denominators cleared (Section~\ref{sec:unwrap}).}\label{tab:unwrap}
  \maybeinput{tables/tab_unwrap.tex}
\end{table}

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \caption{The boundary stress test of Table~\ref{tab:boundarystress},
  repeated with the discrete march at its default tolerance ($0.3$\,rad)
  and with the ODE march at the tight tolerance $10^{-8}$. Bold entries are
  wrong counts. The last column is the number of evaluations of $\Dfun$ the
  discrete march spends on the point.}\label{tab:unwrapstress}
  \maybeinput{tables/tab_unwrap_stress.tex}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:unwrap} measures what Section~\ref{sec:unwrap} predicts. On
the same machine and the same ten thousand points, the discrete march computes
every chart without a single wrong count, $\UwSpeedFourth$, $\UwSpeedShowcase$
and $\UwSpeedTurning$ times faster than the default ODE back-end, and at
$\UwUsFourth$, $\UwUsShowcase$ and $\UwUsTurning$\,$\mu$s per point -- a
complete $100\times100$ chart of any of the three systems in $10$--$35$\,ms.
The gain grows with the difficulty of the system, because the discrete march
pays only for what it must resolve: the median point costs $31$, $52$ and $129$
evaluations of $\Dfun$, whereas the integrators pay for every period of the
delay ripple up to $\wmax$. It is even \emph{faster} than the fixed-step
trapezoid with $500$ steps, which gets $80$--$99\,\%$ of the counts wrong.
The quadrature, which skips the root by-product, is no longer competitive
anywhere. Near a boundary the discrete march is also the more robust
back-end (Table~\ref{tab:unwrapstress}): it reproduces the exact count down
to $|P-P_b|=10^{-12}$ on both sides of the Hopf boundary of the showcase
system, spending at most $165$ evaluations there, while the ODE march at the
tight tolerance $10^{-8}$ misses the narrow peak from $|P-P_b|\le10^{-8}$ on -- the
failure mode discussed above, here resolved by the step rejection on an
unpredicted phase jump and by the root-side decision below the resolution of
the number format.

The discrete march therefore becomes the recommended back-end for charts of
entire characteristic functions, with the ODE march kept for what it does
better: the convergence-controlled integer residual of
Section~\ref{sec:integerness} as an independent check, and rational $\Dfun$
whose denominators cannot be cleared conveniently. All the timings of
Sections~\ref{sec:performance-convergence}--\ref{sec:performance-cost} were
measured with the integration back-ends, on an earlier machine; their
\emph{ratios} remain valid, but the absolute chart times of
Table~\ref{tab:gridtimings} are now one to two orders of magnitude above what
the package delivers by default.

\subsection{GPU sweeps and number formats}\label{sec:performance-gpu}

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \caption{Full-HD brute-force charts ($1920\times1080\approx2.07\cdot10^{6}$
  points, one GPU thread per point) of the fourth-order benchmark with the
  discrete march on four NVIDIA GPUs (Google Colab): median kernel time in
  milliseconds over ten repetitions. $\wmax=10^{5}$ is the production window;
  $\wmax=15$ suffices for this model and is required by Float16, whose range
  ends at $65504$; in the Float16 column $\Dfun$ is evaluated in Float16 on a
  rescaled frequency axis while the march runs in Float32. Float64 and Float32
  give identical charts; the last column is the Float16 throughput in million
  points per second.}\label{tab:gpu}
  \maybeinput{tables/tab_gpu.tex}
\end{table}

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \caption{The discrete march with $\Dfun$ evaluated in narrow number formats
  ($400\times400$ chart of the fourth-order benchmark, $\wmax=15$, best of four
  frequency scalings $\lam=\Omega\mu$ per format; the march itself runs in
  Float32). \emph{Flagged} points carry a sub-resolution or residual flag and
  are re-checked in a wider format; \emph{failed} points did not reach $\wmax$.
  $^\ast$Emulated: every operation is rounded to the format.}\label{tab:precision}
  \maybeinput{tables/tab_precision.tex}
\end{table}

The discrete march is a fixed-size loop of identical scalar steps, so the
parameter points of a chart -- or of any $N$-dimensional point list -- map
one-to-one onto GPU threads. We implemented it as a portable kernel
with \pkg{KernelAbstractions.jl} -- the same code also runs on CPU threads -- that
evaluates a user-written $\Dfun$ with dual numbers, exactly as on the CPU.
Table~\ref{tab:gpu} reports full-HD charts of the fourth-order benchmark. On
the fastest card, the \GpuBest, a complete $1920\times1080$ chart in Float32
takes $\GpuBestFthirtytwo$\,ms -- some seven hundred times the speed of the
twelve-thread CPU of Table~\ref{tab:unwrap}, and identical to the Float64
chart point for point -- and a slider loop that changes the delay and copies
the result back to the host for every frame runs at $\GpuBestSlider$\,ms per
frame. The ranking of the cards follows their single-precision rate:
consumer and graphics-oriented cards run Float64 at $1/32$--$1/64$ of the
Float32 rate, so Float64 is a reference rather than a production format
(only the A100, with full-rate Float64, narrows the gap to a factor of two).
Load balancing turned out not to be an issue: a dynamic work queue, in which
threads that finish early fetch new points, was slower on every card than one
thread per point, because the per-point cost varies only moderately across a chart and the
hardware scheduler absorbs the difference.

Narrower formats pay off only to a point (Table~\ref{tab:precision}). Since
the march needs only the \emph{phase} of $\Dfun$ to a fraction of a radian,
Float16 is accurate enough wherever no root is close to the line: after
rescaling the frequency into its range, it computes the chart another $1.2$--$6$
times faster than Float32 (the gain is largest on cards with a fast
half-precision path) ($\GpuBestFsixteen$\,ms on the \GpuBest), with
$\GpuFsixteenWrong\,\%$ wrong counts that are \emph{all} flagged, and
$\GpuFsixteenFlagged\,\%$ flagged points in total. Re-checking the flagged
points in Float32 restores the exact chart, and done in place on the device
-- gather the flagged points, march them in Float32, scatter the results
back -- the second pass is cheap: for an 8K chart ($7680\times4320\approx
3.3\cdot10^{7}$ points) on the same card, the Float16 sweep takes
$14.8$\,ms and the re-check of its $5.8\cdot10^{5}$ flagged points
$2.6$\,ms, against $43.8$\,ms for the Float32 sweep: an exact chart
$2.5$ times faster than in Float32, and an interactive frame rate at 8K
(\code{gpu/results/interactive\_G4.csv}). The gain depends on the system:
for the lightly damped two-mode turning model a third of the points are
flagged in Float16, and the hybrid ($197$\,ms) is barely faster than Float32
($235$\,ms). BFloat16, with only seven mantissa bits, flags
$14\,\%$ of the chart, and the 8-bit and 4-bit formats are useless for this
purpose: with two or three mantissa bits even the \emph{parameters} of a
chart are quantized to a handful of distinct values per octave, so that
$95$--$100\,\%$ of the points are flagged and up to one in ten counts is
wrong; Float4 gets every count wrong.

\subsection{Comparison with semi-discretization}\label{sec:performance-sd}

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \caption{Comparison with semi-discretization
  (\pkg{SemiDiscretizationMethod.jl}, orders 1 and 2, $n$ steps per delay) on
  the reduced showcase system: error of the dominant characteristic exponent
  and CPU time per parameter point. The reference is the Newton-polished
  dominant root of the quasi-polynomial itself, certified independently of
  either sweep by its recorded defect $|\Dfun(\lam_{\mathrm{ref}})|<10^{-12}$.
  The unrefined rows report the accuracy of the tracked estimate alone; the
  refined rows share the polishing machinery with the reference, so their
  error reflects the polish rather than the sweep.}\label{tab:semidisc}
  \maybeinput{tables/tab_semidisc.tex}
\end{table}

Semi-discretization~\cite{insperger2011semidiscretization} -- here in its
efficient library implementation with the multiplication-free
variant~\cite{bachrathy2026semidisc,bachrathy2026multiplication} --
approximates the dominant characteristic exponent through the spectral
radius of a finite transition matrix, converging polynomially in the number
of discretization steps per delay. Table~\ref{tab:semidisc} compares it with
the present method on the reduced showcase system at a stable, a
near-boundary and an unstable point; the dominant root of the present method
is obtained by the multi-root recipe of Section~\ref{sec:tracking} (ten
tracked minima, all refined, maximal real part). That both converge towards
the same residual-certified reference validates the two formulations against
each other. The substantive difference lies elsewhere: semi-discretization
requires the user to choose the number of steps per delay \emph{by hand},
and an insufficient resolution returns a confidently wrong exponent with no
warning, whereas the present method sets its own frequency steps, exposes
the accuracy through the requested tolerance, and ships two independent
consistency checks (the integer residual and the sign
agreement~\eqref{eq:crosscheck}). The comparison is also intentionally
asymmetric in what the methods deliver: semi-discretization returns the full
dominant multiplier cluster and extends unchanged to time-periodic systems,
where the argument-principle formulation does not apply; the present method
returns exactly what an \emph{autonomous} stability chart needs -- the root
count, the boundary, and the dominant root on demand -- at interactive
speed.

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \caption{Evaluations of $\Dfun$ needed for one parameter point at
  tol~$=10^{-5}$, with the count of each entry verified against a
  tight-tolerance reference before being recorded. What governs the cost is
  how fast the delay-induced ripple of $\theta'$ decays, i.e.\ how far the

  \maybeinput{tables/tab_evals.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:evals} isolates what actually drives the cost. The decisive
quantity is not the back-end but the decay rate of the phase ripple, set by
how far the delayed terms sit below the leading power of $\Dfun$. Where the
ripple decays fast (delayed \emph{position} feedback,
$\theta'\sim\omega^{-3}$), quadrature recognizes the negligible tail panel
by panel and finishes about $2.5$ times cheaper than the march; where it
decays slowly (delayed \emph{velocity} feedback, $\theta'\sim\omega^{-1}$),
the ranking reverses and the march is some three times cheaper. Neither
back-end dominates, and the lesson for a user is to spend attention on
$\wmax$ and on the structure of the feedback, not on the choice of
integrator. One implementation detail underpins the quadrature numbers: the
Gauss--Kronrod back-end is seeded with logarithmically spaced interior
breakpoints, one per two frequency decades. Without them the first
$G_7$--$K_{15}$ panel spans the entire window, its lowest node lands above
the whole resonance region for $\wmax=10^{6}$, and the routine returns
$\Zraw=n/2$ having never seen a single root -- with an integer residual of
\emph{exactly} zero, since nothing about the quadrature failed. The
breakpoints cost a few dozen extra evaluations and remove the failure mode
entirely.

Figure~\ref{fig:zoo} extends the comparison to a wider selection of ODE
solvers on the fourth-order benchmark system. The fixed-step trapezoid is
\emph{dominated everywhere}: even at $3\cdot10^{4}$ steps -- more than an
order of magnitude slower than the adaptive default -- its worst error over
the chart remains several roots, because a uniform grid cannot resolve a
peak that narrows without bound as a root approaches the line. All adaptive
methods follow their tolerance; higher-order pairs dominate at tight
tolerances, and the linearly implicit pair is uniformly dominated -- there
is no stiffness for it to exploit. The spread between the \emph{adaptive}
methods is far smaller than the gap that separates all of them from a fixed
grid, which is the practical message of this section.
