\section{Conclusions and outlook}\label{sec:conclusion}

The Nyquist/argument-principle criterion for linear autonomous time-delay
systems was reformulated as a scalar ordinary differential equation for the
phase of the characteristic function and marched by a high-order adaptive
integrator with an exact, automatically differentiated integrand. The
reformulation tames the practically unbounded frequency range and the
near-singular integrand through the step-size control of modern ODE
software, and the integer residual of the computed winding number gives a
dimensionless error measure whose largest value over a chart follows the
requested tolerance as $\max\varepsilon\approx100\,\mathrm{tol}$. The phase
ODE carries no stiffness -- its right-hand side is independent of the state,
so it is a quadrature in disguise -- and what the ordered march provides
beyond quadrature is the characteristic root obtained along the way: a smooth
first-order estimate at negligible cost, sharpened by an optional Taylor,
Newton or combined polish when an accurate root is wanted.

Since the count needs only the end value of the phase, the integration can
be dropped. The discrete phase-unwrapping march sums the exact phase
increments between adaptively placed samples, and its step control, driven
by the exact slope, rejects steps that hide a root transition; transitions
below the resolution of the number format are decided by the side of the
tracked root. With one evaluation per step and $30$--$130$ evaluations for a
median point it computes the benchmark charts
$\UwSpeedFourth$--$\UwSpeedTurning$ times faster than the phase ODE without a
single wrong count, and it resolves the Hopf boundary of the showcase down to
a parameter distance of $10^{-12}$. Its cost is best described per thread:
$\ParCpuOneUs\,\mu$s per point on one CPU thread for the fourth-order
benchmark ($\ParGpuOneUsLo$--$\ParGpuOneUsHi\,\mu$s on one GPU thread in
Float32), divided almost linearly among CPU threads ($\ParCpuFortyEightSpeedup$
times on $48$ hardware threads) and, on GPUs, in proportion to $1/n$ over
three decades of thread counts at an efficiency of about $0.3$--$0.4$, up to
the effective parallelism of the device. The fastest of the four GPUs
measured thus computes a full-HD chart in under a millisecond and an 8K chart
in about ten milliseconds of kernel time, fast enough to recompute it while a
slider is moved, and the model estimates the chart time on another NVIDIA
card from its core count within a factor of about two. On the benchmark,
Float32 gives the Float64 chart except at a single flagged point; Float16 is
accurate with all its rare errors flagged, but not faster, and 8-bit and 4-bit
formats are useless for stability charts. The discrete march is the tool for
charts, real-time exploration and parameter optimization; the ODE march
remains the reference, with its convergence-controlled residual.

The root estimate transforms the integer-valued root-counting map into an
interpolable chart: one coloring simultaneously shows the number of unstable
roots and the stability margin of the stable domain; the estimated root distance
serves as a scalar objective for the multi-dimensional bisection method,
which traces the true stability boundary (rather than all root-crossing
D-curves) at high equivalent resolution; the most robust parameter point
follows from the largest inscribed circle; and shifted integration lines
yield asymptotic decay-rate ($\gamma$-stability) contours. The
characteristic function itself is extracted algorithmically from a
simulation-ready right-hand side -- including nonlinear models (linearized
automatically) and singular mass matrices -- so the complete
workflow requires nothing beyond the model and the parameter ranges. The
case studies demonstrate the identical code path on retarded, neutral,
distributed-delay, differential--algebraic, transcendental,
high-dimensional and fractional-order systems.

One modeling lesson deserves to be carried away from the case studies,
because it costs nothing and outweighs any tuning of the numerics: where a
model admits a natural open-loop/feedback split, the characteristic function
should be written as the return difference $1+L(\lam)$ rather than as a raw
determinant. The zeros are the same, the added poles are the open-loop roots
and lie in the left half-plane for an open-loop stable structure (otherwise
the open-loop unstable poles must be added to the count), and the leading
order becomes exactly zero -- which removes both the need to estimate it and
the astronomical magnitudes that a high-order determinant produces. For the
discrete march the denominators must be cleared again
(Section~\ref{sec:unwrap}), so the two back-ends prefer different forms.

The limitations delineate the outlook. First, the method is restricted to
autonomous (time-invariant) linear systems: for time-periodic coefficients
the characteristic function would have to be replaced by a Hill-type
infinite determinant, and time-domain discretization methods such as
semi-discretization~\cite{insperger2011semidiscretization,bachrathy2026multiplication}
remain the more practical choice there. Second, away from the stability
boundary the rightmost-root estimate is of first order unless refined, so
the method complements rather than replaces full spectral solvers when the
deep spectrum is needed. Third -- intrinsic to the argument principle
itself -- a peak that no sample lands on shifts the count by one while
leaving it an integer, so the residual cannot see it; the cross-check
between the count and the independently tracked root detects this at no
cost, the optional detect-and-repair callback of
Section~\ref{sec:showcase-tolerance} mends the flagged pixels within the
march, a free parity test detects a single skipped peak, and the discrete
march rejects such steps by construction -- its own blind spot being a pair
of transitions in the same direction within one step, which an entire
$\Dfun$ and a band-limited step cap removed on the benchmarks but which near
a double-Hopf point remains possible. A formally guaranteed count would
still require a different device, such as interval
arithmetic~\cite{johnson2009enclosing}. Fourth, for neutral systems beyond the
essential instability threshold the root count diverges with the truncation
frequency; the classification into stable and unstable domains nevertheless
remains correct (Appendix~\ref{app:gallery}). Fifth, the fractional-order
results are preliminary: the counting on the principal branch was validated
numerically -- including the reproduction of a published
fractional-controller design chart -- but a rigorous treatment of
branch-cut effects, in particular for shifted integration lines, is left
for future work. Sixth, on neutral systems the cost of the discrete march
grows linearly with $\wmax$, so the window must be chosen explicitly there,
and a quantitative comparison with spectral collocation of the infinitesimal
generator, the strongest time-domain competitor for autonomous systems, is
still to be made. Finally, for large models the cost is dominated by each
evaluation of $\Dfun$ rather than by the method; where the feedback has low
rank the matrix determinant lemma already removes most of that
cost~\eqref{eq:detlemma}, and caching factorizations across frequencies,
extending the automatic extraction to neutral right-hand sides,
evaluating large determinants on the GPU, and coupling the real-time charts
to formal optimization loops and to a browser front end are natural
continuations.

\section*{Software and reproducibility}
The method is implemented in the open-source Julia package
\pkg{InterpolatedNyquist.jl} (MIT license,
\url{https://github.com/bachrathyd/InterpolatedNyquist.jl}), built on
\pkg{OrdinaryDiffEq.jl}~\cite{rackauckas2017differentialequations},
\pkg{ForwardDiff.jl}~\cite{revels2016forward},
\pkg{QuadGK.jl}~\cite{johnson2013quadgk},
\pkg{MDBM.jl}~\cite{bachrathy2025mdbm} and
\pkg{DelaunayTriangulation.jl}~\cite{vandenheuvel2024delaunay}. The GPU implementation of the
discrete march (\pkg{NyquistGPU}, with a Google Colab notebook) is
in the \code{gpu/} directory of the same repository. All figures and tables of this
paper can be regenerated by the scripts in the \code{paper/} directory of the
repository; the benchmark hardware and software versions are embedded
automatically in the manuscript.
