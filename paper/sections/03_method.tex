\section{Ordered adaptive integration with rightmost-root tracking}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The winding integral as an ordinary differential equation}\label{sec:phaseode}

The key step is disarmingly simple: instead of applying quadrature
to~\eqref{eq:count}, the accumulated phase is treated as the state of the
scalar initial value problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phaseode}
  y'(\omega) = \theta'(\omega)
             = \Imag\frac{\Dfun'(\omega)}{\Dfun(\sigma+\ii\omega)},
  \qquad y(0)=0,
  \qquad
  \Zraw = \frac{n}{2}-\frac{y(\wmax)}{\pi},
  \qquad
  \Zint = \operatorname{round}(\Zraw),
\end{equation}
which is handed to a modern adaptive integrator with local error control.
It should be stated plainly what this formulation is and is not. The
right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:phaseode} does not depend on the state $y$, so
the Jacobian $\partial_y y'$ vanishes identically: \eqref{eq:phaseode} is a
quadrature problem in disguise, it is never stiff in the classical sense,
and an automatically stiffness-switching integrator provably never switches
on it. What the integrator contributes is therefore not stiffness handling
but \emph{adaptive step-size control on an ordered march}: steps contract
around the near-singular peaks~\eqref{eq:peak}, where the phase turns by
$\pm\pi$ over a frequency interval of width $|\sigma_r-\sigma|$, and expand
again over the smooth stretches. Because the integrand is analytic, a
high-order explicit Runge--Kutta pair is the appropriate choice; the default
is \code{Vern9}~\cite{verner2010numerically}, which at loose tolerances is
three orders of magnitude more accurate than the fifth-order
alternative~\cite{tsitouras2011runge} and at tight tolerances is also
faster (Section~\ref{sec:performance-convergence}). The ordered march is
what makes the root tracking of Section~\ref{sec:tracking} possible, and
that -- not stiffness -- is the reason the phase ODE is preferred here over
plain quadrature.

The integrand is evaluated \emph{exactly}: the characteristic function is called
once per frequency with a dual-number argument
$\lam = \sigma + \ii(\omega+\delta)$, $\delta^2=0$ (distinct from the
integer residual $\varepsilon$ of Section~\ref{sec:integerness}), so
forward-mode automatic differentiation~\cite{revels2016forward} delivers
$\Dfun$ and $\Dfun'=\partial_\omega\Dfun$ simultaneously, without finite
differencing and without any user-supplied derivative. A tiny offset
$\omega\mapsto\max(\omega,10^{-9})$ regularizes branch points of fractional
terms at the origin.

Two practical parameters remain. The truncation frequency $\wmax$ can be
chosen generously: past the highest structural resonance the phase slope
decays algebraically, the adaptive step size grows accordingly, and a
\emph{too small} $\wmax$ is immediately visible in the result, because the
truncated tail shifts $\Zraw$ away from an integer
(Section~\ref{sec:integerness}). The cost of the tail is governed by the
decay of the delay-induced phase ripple: if the delayed terms sit at least
two orders below the leading power of $\Dfun$ -- as for delayed
\emph{position} feedback on mechanical systems -- the ripple decays at least
like $\omega^{-2}$, and pushing $\wmax$ far beyond the resonances is
essentially free: for the fourth-order benchmark the march costs $1426$,
$1474$ and $1506$ evaluations of $\Dfun$ at $\wmax=10^{6}$, $10^{8}$ and
$10^{10}$ respectively -- four extra decades of frequency for six percent
more work, with $\Zraw$ unchanged to seven digits. One caution applies to those giant
steps: an adaptive error estimator only \emph{samples} the local error, so a
step that grows to many ripple periods can in principle alias a slowly
decaying oscillation; in practice the embedded error estimate of a
high-order pair rejects such steps long before the count is affected, and
any surviving aliasing is exposed by the integer residual of
Section~\ref{sec:integerness}. If a delayed term
sits only one order below the leading power (delayed \emph{velocity}
feedback), the ripple decays merely like $\omega^{-1}$, the solver keeps
resolving it (Fig.~\ref{fig:walkthrough}c), and every resolved step
contributes its share of local error; there $\wmax$ does cost, and the
integer-residual indicator of Section~\ref{sec:integerness} exposes both a
truncated tail and an excessively long march at a glance.

The resulting recommendation is asymmetric, and deliberately so.
\emph{For accuracy, be generous}: $\wmax=10^{6}$ is a safe default for every
system in this paper, and $10^{8}$ costs almost nothing extra where the
ripple decays quickly. \emph{For speed, be frugal}: two to three decades
above the highest resonance ($\wmax=10^{4}$ for the mechanical systems here)
leaves a truncated tail of order $0.2/\wmax$ in $\Zraw$ -- five orders of
magnitude below the rounding threshold -- and, for a slowly decaying ripple,
cuts the evaluation count by more than an order of magnitude. The charts of
Section~\ref{sec:showcase} exploit the first setting; the gallery of
Appendix~\ref{app:gallery} exploits the second. Since a too-small $\wmax$
announces itself in the residual, the choice can be made empirically rather
than by analysis.

The leading order $n$ in~\eqref{eq:phaseode} is estimated numerically, and
the natural place to do so is not the imaginary axis at all. The term $n/2$
accounts for the phase gathered on the arc at infinity, and that arc lies in
the \emph{right half-plane}, where every delayed factor $\ee^{-\lam\tau_j}$
decays exponentially. Probing along the positive real axis therefore
measures precisely the quantity the counting formula needs, with the delay
terms already extinguished:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:npow}
  n \;\approx\; \operatorname*{LS\text{-}slope}_{\;s_k\in[s_\ast/10,\;s_\ast]}
  \Bigl(\ln s_k \,\mapsto\, \ln\bigl|\Dfun(s_k)\bigr|\Bigr),
  \qquad s_\ast \gg 1 ,
\end{equation}
the least-squares slope of the log--log magnitude over a handful of
log-spaced real samples. Two points about $s_\ast$ deserve emphasis. It is
\emph{decoupled from the integration range}: $n$ is a property of $\Dfun$
alone, so the probe may sit far beyond $\wmax$ -- the default is
$s_\ast=10^{8}$ whatever $\wmax$ is -- and it costs a handful of evaluations
once per sweep, not once per frequency. And on the real axis a larger
$s_\ast$ genuinely buys accuracy: $|\Dfun(s)|\to|c|\,s^{n}$ with a smooth
algebraic correction, so the estimate converges as $O(1/s_\ast)$, measured
here as $7.5\cdot10^{-5}$, $7.4\cdot10^{-7}$ and $7.4\cdot10^{-9}$ at
$s_\ast=10^{4}$, $10^{6}$ and $10^{8}$ -- including the effective integer
order of neutral equations and the non-integer order of fractional ones.

That last point is exactly what an imaginary-axis fit cannot offer, and the
contrast explains why the difficulty is worth side-stepping rather than
fighting. On the imaginary axis $|\ee^{-\ii\omega\tau}|=1$, so the delay
terms superimpose a ripple on $\ln|\Dfun(\ii\omega)|$ that a log-spaced fit
aliases rather than averages; for a neutral system, where the ripple does not
decay at all, \emph{no} frequency is large enough, and a single-point
evaluation of $\omega\,\Real(\Dfun'/\Dfun)$ does not merely stall but
diverges in proportion to $\omega$ (measured: $n_{\mathrm{est}}=-226$, $5132$
and $80\,028$ at $\omega=10^{3}$, $10^{4}$ and $10^{6}$ for a system whose
true effective order is $2$). Even for retarded systems the same estimator
retains an error of $O(b\tau/c)$ at arbitrarily large $\omega$ when a delayed
term sits one order below the leading power ($b$ the delayed-term
coefficient, $c$ the leading one). Probing where the delays are
exponentially small removes all of this at a stroke. The estimator needs no
structural knowledge of the model, costs a handful of extra evaluations, and
in parameter sweeps is computed once when $n$ is parameter-independent.

Two limitations are worth naming. The first is representability:
$|\Dfun(s)|\sim s^{n}$ overflows for large $n$, so $s_\ast$ is reduced
automatically until $|\Dfun|$ is finite, which for very high-order
determinants caps the achievable accuracy; the leading order may then be
supplied explicitly. There is, however, a better answer than fighting the
symptom, and it costs nothing. Whenever the model offers a natural
open-loop/feedback split -- as every control problem and every
regenerative-machining model does -- the \emph{return difference}
$\Dfun=\det(\mat I+\mat Q_0^{-1}\mat F)=1+L(\lam)$ (with $\mat F$ the
feedback matrix) has exactly the same zeros
as $\det(\mat Q_0+\mat F)$, adds poles only at the open-loop roots (which, for
an open-loop stable structure, lie in the left half-plane and leave the
right-half-plane count untouched), and tends to $1$ at infinity. Then $n=0$
\emph{exactly}, no estimate is needed, and the enormous magnitudes disappear:
for the $29$-DOF finite element bar of Appendix~\ref{app:gallery} the raw determinant
grows like $\lam^{58}$, reaches $\sim\FemRawMag$ at the top of the integration range, and leaves an integer
residual of $\FemRawResid$ -- a rounded count barely better than a coin toss
-- while the return difference leaves $\FemRdResid$. Where the split exists,
it is worth more than any tuning of the integrator.

The second limitation is more interesting.
Equation~\eqref{eq:npow} presumes that $\Dfun$ is polynomially bounded in
the right half-plane, which holds for every quasi-polynomial
$\Dfun=\sum_j a_j\lam^{k_j}\ee^{-\lam\tau_j}$ with $\tau_j\ge0$ -- hence for
all retarded, neutral and differential--algebraic systems -- but not for the
transcendental characteristic functions of continua, where
$\cosh\bigl(\sqrt{\lam^2+c\lam}\,L\bigr)$ grows like $\ee^{\lam L}$ and no
finite leading power exists on the real axis at all. The package detects this
automatically -- for a genuine power law the fitted slope must be the same
one window lower, and for an exponentially growing $\Dfun$ it is not -- and
falls back to a least-squares fit along the imaginary axis, where such
functions are bounded and oscillatory and the fit returns the effective order
the counting formula needs ($n\approx0$ for the beam of Appendix~\ref{app:gallery}).
The fallback inherits the delay ripple and is correspondingly less accurate,
which is precisely why it is not the primary route.

The requested relative and absolute solver tolerances control the local
error of the accumulated phase, measured in radians. Since the exact
winding number is an integer multiple of $\pi$ in the phase, the error of
$\Zraw$ inherits the tolerance directly as a \emph{dimensionless} quantity:
the same default ($10^{-5}$) is appropriate for a machining model and for a
dense $50\times50$ matrix determinant alike, with no problem-dependent
scaling (Section~\ref{sec:integerness}).

\subsection{Rightmost-root estimation as a by-product}\label{sec:tracking}

The peaks of the integrand are not only a numerical nuisance -- they carry
the information where the dominant characteristic roots are. Along the
integration line, $|\Dfun(\sigma+\ii\omega)|^{2}$ dips to a sharp local
minimum at $\omega\approx\omega_r$ for every nearby root
$\lam_r=\sigma_r+\ii\omega_r$, and the smallest minimum belongs to the root
closest to the line, which for $\sigma=0$ is typically the rightmost root
that decides stability. The solver therefore tracks
$\min_\omega|\Dfun|^{2}$ during the march at negligible cost: at every
integrand evaluation the already computed quantities $\Dfun$ and $\Dfun'$
yield the real part of one Newton step towards the root,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigmaest}
  \sest \;=\; \sigma + \Real\!\left(-\frac{\Dfun}{\dd\Dfun/\dd\lam}\right)
        \;=\; \sigma - \frac{\Real\Dfun\cdot\partial_\omega\Imag\Dfun
              - \Imag\Dfun\cdot\partial_\omega\Real\Dfun}{|\Dfun'|^{2}},
\end{equation}
using $\dd\Dfun/\dd\lam=-\ii\,\Dfun'$, and the frequency of the minimum is
stored as $\west$. The pair $(\sest,\west)$ is a first-order estimate of the
dominant root location returned together with $\Zint$; its accuracy is
highest exactly at the stability boundary, where the root lies on the line
and the Newton step becomes exact up to the solver tolerance. Away from the
boundary the estimate degrades gracefully, but there only its sign and
magnitude matter -- as a smooth measure of the distance to instability.

Several roots can be tracked simultaneously. A compile-time variant of the
solver detects every local minimum of $|\Dfun|^{2}$ through the sign change
of its $\omega$-derivative, $2(\Real\Dfun\,\partial_\omega\Real\Dfun +
\Imag\Dfun\,\partial_\omega\Imag\Dfun)$, and maintains the $N_r$ best
candidates in a sorted, allocation-free buffer; a boundary test at
$\omega=0$ captures real dominant roots, since $|\Dfun|^{2}$ is even in
$\omega$. Tracking one root is the default and adds a few percent to the sweep
time; tracking
zero roots gives maximal sweep speed. One caveat deserves emphasis: the
\emph{deepest} minimum of $|\Dfun|$ belongs to the rightmost root near the
stability boundary, but deeper inside the stable or unstable domains a
different root branch may produce a deeper dip. When the true dominant root
is required away from the boundary, several minima should be tracked
($N_r\approx5$--$10$), all of them refined, and the dominant root taken as
the one with the maximal real part -- this recipe is validated against an
independent eigenvalue method in Section~\ref{sec:performance-sd}.

That recommendation is worth more than a footnote, because the single-minimum
estimate is not merely inaccurate away from the boundary: it is
\emph{discontinuous}. Where one branch overtakes another as the closest one,
$\west$ jumps between modes and $\sest$ jumps with it -- on the showcase
system, from $-0.194$ to $-0.053$ as $\west$ jumps from $0.96$ to $2.34$,
across a single step in the gain. Any quantity built on it inherits the jump,
and a method that fits a local linear model -- as the boundary tracer of
Section~\ref{sec:mdbm} does inside each cell -- will read a steep gradient as
a zero crossing and place a spurious boundary point in the middle of the
stable domain. The dominant root passes through a branch crossing smoothly,
because it is a maximum over branches rather than the nearest one, so
building the coloring and the boundary objective from it removes the artefact
at its source. For the count itself the caveat is immaterial, since near the
boundary the crossing root always produces the global minimum.

\subsection{Higher-order root refinement}\label{sec:refinement}

The tracked estimate $(\sest,\west)$ is refined by local corrections built
from the same automatic derivatives. Writing the Taylor expansion at
$\hat\lam=\sest+\ii\west$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:taylor}
  \Dfun(\hat\lam+\Delta) \approx \Dfun + \Dfun_\lam\,\Delta
   + \tfrac12 \Dfun_{\lam\lam}\,\Delta^{2}
   + \tfrac16 \Dfun_{\lam\lam\lam}\,\Delta^{3} = 0,
\end{equation}
where the subscripts denote derivatives with respect to $\lam$ evaluated at
$\hat\lam$, the package offers (i) \emph{Newton--Raphson} iterations
$\Delta=-\Dfun/\Dfun_\lam$ (four steps by default); (ii) a single
\emph{quadratic} (Poly-2) or (iii) \emph{cubic} (Poly-3) Taylor solve, where
the root of the local cubic closest to $\hat\lam$ is selected via the
eigenvalues of its companion matrix. The first derivative is exact (automatic
differentiation); the higher derivatives are obtained by central differences
of the exact first derivative, which avoids the compilation cost of nested
dual numbers while keeping truncation errors far below the achievable
accuracy. An optional mode freezes $\west$ and corrects only the real part,
which is convenient when the frequency is pinned by measurement. The
accuracy and cost of the variants are quantified in
Section~\ref{sec:showcase-roots}; the practical summary is that every
variant costs a few percent of the phase integration, that Newton is
simultaneously the cheapest and the most accurate -- hence the default --
and that a few steps converge to machine precision near the
boundary.\footnote{Like any local root iteration, neither correction is
guaranteed to converge: a Newton step can leave the basin of the intended
root, a Taylor root can be spurious when the seed is poor, and at a
defective (multiple) root, where $\Dfun_\lam\to0$, Newton's convergence
degrades from quadratic to linear. The implementation therefore rejects
runaway steps, which suffices in every case study of this paper; should a
chart nevertheless show isolated irregular root estimates, refinement can be
switched off (\code{refinement\_method=:Linear}) to fall back to the raw
tracked estimate, whose sign -- the quantity the charts rely on -- is
unaffected.} If several estimates are tracked, the
same refinement applied to each returns a set of dominant roots, comparable
to the output of dedicated root finders such as
QPmR~\cite{vyhlidal2009mapping} -- with the difference that here the root
solve is a cheap post-processing step of the stability count.

\subsection{Self-validating error estimate}\label{sec:integerness}

The exact winding number is an integer, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eps}
  \varepsilon = \bigl|\Zraw - \operatorname{round}(\Zraw)\bigr|
\end{equation}
is a rigorous, freely available indicator of the accumulated numerical
error: it contains the local integration errors, the tail truncated at
$\wmax$, and the residual of the estimated order~\eqref{eq:npow}. Since the
phase is integrated in radians, $\varepsilon$ is \emph{dimensionless} and
scales essentially linearly with the requested tolerance -- amplified by
the accepted-step count, since the per-step error tolerance accumulates
along the march -- hence the tolerance acts as a universal accuracy dial
that transfers between systems without re-tuning. In the numerical
experiments of Section~\ref{sec:showcase-tolerance}, $\varepsilon$ tracks
the tolerance across six decades: at $10^{-2}$ the residual reaches order
one and genuine miscounts appear -- the deliberate failure demonstration of
Fig.~\ref{fig:errorfield} -- while $10^{-4}$ already keeps $\varepsilon$ an
order of magnitude below the rounding threshold $1/2$ and produces reliable
charts at near-maximal speed, and $10^{-8}$ yields reference-quality values
at a moderate CPU-time premium; the package default $10^{-5}$ adds a further
safety decade at little cost.

\paragraph{Supplying the leading order.} The order $n$ is an \emph{input} of
the counting formula~\eqref{eq:count}, not a result of it, and the estimator
of Section~\ref{sec:extraction} exists only so that a user who does not know
$n$ is not blocked. Whenever $n$ is known -- and it usually is, by inspection
of the model -- it should simply be passed
(\code{n\_power\_max}), for every back-end and for a whole chart at once. It is
then exact rather than estimated, and it costs nothing. This matters most
exactly where the estimator is least comfortable: a neutral $\Dfun =
\lam^{2}(1+a\ee^{-\lam})+\dots$ has $n=2$ by inspection, because
$\ee^{-s}\to0$ along the real axis, yet on the imaginary axis $|\Dfun|$ never
settles onto a power law -- it oscillates by a factor
$(1+|a|)/(1-|a|)$, up to $39$ at $|a|=0.95$, for ever. The estimator does
recover $n=2$ there (to all printed digits, on every neutral case study of
Appendix~\ref{app:gallery}), but the user has no way to know that in advance,
and one declared number removes the doubt. The same applies to fractional
orders, which are legitimate non-integer inputs.

\paragraph{When the residual stops being a check.} The diagnostic has a
precondition that deserves to be stated rather than discovered, and -- as the
neutral case shows -- it is neither the tolerance nor the leading order, but
the \emph{convergence of the tail}. Equation~\eqref{eq:eps} measures the distance of $\Zraw$ from an
integer, and $\Zraw$ is only \emph{expected} to be an integer when the
truncated remainder $\int_{\wmax}^{\infty}$ is negligible. For retarded
systems it is, and $\varepsilon$ behaves as described: across the retarded
case studies of Appendix~\ref{app:gallery} the median residual is
$\sim10^{-4}$ and essentially no grid point is uncertain. For \emph{neutral}
systems it is not. There the integrand does not decay at all, the tail
contributes an oscillation bounded by $\arcsin|a|/\pi$ rather than a
vanishing remainder (Section~\ref{sec:classes}), and the residual inherits
it: on the neutral panels of Appendix~\ref{app:gallery} the median residual
is $\approx0.1$ and between $10\%$ and $27\%$ of grid points exceed the
$1/4$ mark -- with the counts nevertheless correct. For that class,
therefore, $\varepsilon$ is \emph{not} a usable self-check, and what protects
the count is the analytic bound $\arcsin|a|/\pi<1/2$ instead. Enlarging
$\wmax$ does not help and should not be attempted: the oscillation is
bounded, not decaying.

One caveat is inherited from the argument principle itself and deserves to
be stated sharply, because it bounds what $\varepsilon$ can certify. A march
that steps \emph{over} a peak loses exactly $\pm\pi$ of phase, so $\Zraw$
shifts by exactly one and remains just as close to an integer as before:
$\varepsilon$ measures the \emph{quadrature} quality and is structurally
blind to a skipped peak. Nor is this hypothetical: approaching a stability
boundary the peak half-width $|\sigma_r-\sigma|$ collapses to many orders of
magnitude below any practical step size (quantified in
Table~\ref{tab:boundarystress}), and the marching solver then strides
past it and reports success. No pointwise method is immune: quadrature
rules that happen to place all their nodes outside such a spike are equally
blind, and only survive here because the delay ripple forces them to
subdivide finely everywhere anyway
(Section~\ref{sec:performance-convergence}).

What rescues the situation is the second output of the same sweep. The
tracked root of Section~\ref{sec:tracking} is obtained from the minima of
$|\Dfun|^{2}$, a mechanism entirely independent of the accumulated phase,
and it remains accurate exactly where the peak is narrow -- that is, exactly
where the count is fragile. The two therefore cross-validate each other: a
skipped peak shows up as an \emph{inconsistency} between $\operatorname{sign}
(\sest-\sigma)$ and the predicate $\Zint=0$; both quantities are standard
outputs of every sweep, so the check costs one comparison.

The test must, however, be restricted to a neighbourhood of the line, and the
reason is instructive. Far from the boundary the tracked minimum need not
belong to the \emph{dominant} root (Section~\ref{sec:tracking}): deep inside
an unstable domain the roots that make $\Zint>0$ may sit far to the right and
leave no dip near the line at all, so the tracked root is a stable one and
disagrees with $\Zint>0$ entirely legitimately. Applied blindly, the test
therefore fires on \CheckNaiveRef{} of the \CheckNpoints{} points of a
\emph{fully converged} chart of the showcase system, which would make it
worthless. A peak can only be stepped over where a root lies close to the
line, so the informative test is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crosscheck}
  \operatorname{sign}(\sest-\sigma)\ \text{disagrees with}\ [\Zint=0]
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad |\sest-\sigma| < h ,
\end{equation}
with $h$ a small fraction of the spread of $\sest$ over the chart. With
$h=\CheckH$ -- orders of magnitude below the median $|\sest|\approx
\CheckMedianSigma$ of those spurious disagreements -- the test fires on only
\CheckNearRef{} of the same \CheckNpoints{} points, and it catches
\CheckLooseCaught{} of the \CheckLooseFlips{} miscounts that a deliberately
loose run ($\mathrm{tol}=\CheckLooseTol$) produces in the only place that
matters (Section~\ref{sec:showcase-tolerance}). That last qualification is the point:
skipping one peak only ever changes $\Zint$ by one, so the stable/unstable
classification a chart displays is affected only where the last unstable root
crosses -- and that is precisely the regime in which $\sest$ is small and the
test is sharp. The same mechanism that flags a skipped peak can also mend
it: the minimum of $|\Dfun|^{2}$ \emph{locates} the peak precisely however
narrow it is, and Section~\ref{sec:showcase-tolerance} shows how an
optional callback uses that location to repair the count within the march
itself.

\subsection{Alternative integrators within the same framework}\label{sec:alternatives}

The phase integrand~\eqref{eq:integrand} can also be fed to classical
adaptive quadrature; the package provides a Gauss--Kronrod back-end
(\pkg{QuadGK.jl}~\cite{johnson2013quadgk}) with the identical interface.
For the root count alone it is fully competitive: it stays within a factor of
about three of the march in evaluations either way -- cheaper when the phase
ripple decays quickly, because a quadrature rule can recognize a negligible
tail and stop where a march must still walk to $\wmax$, dearer when the
ripple persists (Table~\ref{tab:evals}) -- and close to a stability boundary
it is the more dependable of the two
(Section~\ref{sec:performance-convergence}). It is a reasonable choice
whenever only $\Zint$ is wanted. The decisive difference concerns the rightmost root.
Adaptive quadrature subdivides its interval recursively and therefore visits
the frequencies in an essentially arbitrary order: there is no sweep along
which a local minimum of $|\Dfun|^{2}$ could be recognized, and the
bookkeeping that the ODE march gets for free would require a separate
minimization. It is this ordered traversal -- not accuracy, not speed, and
certainly not stiffness -- that earns the phase ODE its place as the
default, because every chart in this paper is built from the tracked root:
the interpolable coloring~\eqref{eq:coloring}, the boundary
objective~\eqref{eq:objective}, the refinement seeds, and the consistency
check of Section~\ref{sec:integerness} all consume $\sest$, not $\Zint$
alone. A third back-end evaluates the integral by a fixed-step trapezoidal rule with
no adaptivity and essentially zero overhead. It is included for completeness
and as a real-time floor, but the measurements of
Section~\ref{sec:performance-zoo} do not support recommending it for the
systems studied here, and it is worth saying why: a \emph{uniform} frequency
grid cannot resolve the peak~\eqref{eq:peak} of a root that lies close to the
line, and near a stability boundary that peak is arbitrarily narrow. Its mean
count error therefore converges with the step count while its \emph{worst}
error over a chart does not -- at $3\cdot10^{4}$ steps, more than an order of
magnitude slower than the adaptive default, individual points are still off
by several roots. Adaptivity is thus not a convenience here but the very
mechanism that makes the count trustworthy, which is the same reason the
step-size control was introduced in Section~\ref{sec:phaseode}.
