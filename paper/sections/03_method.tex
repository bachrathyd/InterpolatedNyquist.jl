\section{Ordered adaptive integration with rightmost-root tracking}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The winding integral as an ordinary differential equation}\label{sec:phaseode}

The key step is simple: instead of applying quadrature
to~\eqref{eq:count}, the accumulated phase is treated as the state of the
scalar initial value problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phaseode}
  y'(\omega) = \theta'(\omega)
             = \Imag\frac{\Dfun'(\omega)}{\Dfun(\sigma+\ii\omega)},
  \qquad y(0)=0,
  \qquad
  \Zraw = \frac{n}{2}-\frac{y(\wmax)}{\pi},
  \qquad
  \Zint = \operatorname{round}(\Zraw),
\end{equation}
which is handed to a modern adaptive integrator with local error control.
It should be stated plainly what this formulation is and is not. The
right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:phaseode} does not depend on the state $y$, so
the Jacobian $\partial_y y'$ vanishes identically: \eqref{eq:phaseode} is a
quadrature problem in disguise, it is never stiff in the classical sense,
and an automatically stiffness-switching integrator provably never switches
on it. What the integrator contributes is therefore not stiffness handling
but \emph{adaptive step-size control on an ordered march}: steps contract
around the near-singular peaks~\eqref{eq:peak}, where the phase turns by
$\pm\pi$ over a frequency interval of width $|\sigma_r-\sigma|$, and expand
again over the smooth stretches. Because the integrand is analytic, a
high-order explicit Runge--Kutta pair is the appropriate choice; the default
is \code{Vern9}~\cite{verner2010numerically}, which at the default tolerance
is three orders of magnitude more accurate than the fifth-order
alternative~\cite{tsitouras2011runge} for a factor of $2.4$ in cost
(Section~\ref{sec:performance-convergence}). The ordered march is
what makes the root tracking of Section~\ref{sec:tracking} possible, which
is the reason the phase ODE is preferred here over plain quadrature
(Section~\ref{sec:alternatives}).

The integrand is evaluated \emph{exactly}: the characteristic function is called
once per frequency with a dual-number argument
$\lam = \sigma + \ii(\omega+\delta)$, $\delta^2=0$, so
forward-mode automatic differentiation~\cite{revels2016forward} delivers
$\Dfun$ and $\Dfun'=\partial_\omega\Dfun$ simultaneously, without finite
differencing and without any user-supplied derivative. A tiny offset
$\omega\mapsto\max(\omega,10^{-9})$ regularizes branch points of fractional
terms at the origin.

Two practical parameters remain. The truncation frequency $\wmax$ can be
chosen generously: a \emph{too small} $\wmax$ is visible in the result,
because the truncated tail shifts $\Zraw$ away from an integer
(Section~\ref{sec:integerness}). The cost of the tail is set by the decay of
the delay-induced phase ripple. If the delayed terms sit at least two orders
below the leading power of $\Dfun$ (delayed \emph{position} feedback), the
ripple decays at least like $\omega^{-2}$ and a wide window is essentially
free: the fourth-order benchmark costs $1426$, $1474$ and $1506$ evaluations
at $\wmax=10^{6}$, $10^{8}$ and $10^{10}$. If a delayed term sits only one
order below (delayed \emph{velocity} feedback), the ripple decays like
$\omega^{-1}$, the solver keeps resolving it (Fig.~\ref{fig:walkthrough}c),
and $\wmax$ does cost. Hence: \emph{for accuracy, be generous}
($\wmax=10^{6}$ is safe for every system in this paper); \emph{for speed, be
frugal} -- two to three decades above the highest resonance leave a tail of
order $0.2/\wmax$ in $\Zraw$, far below the rounding threshold.

The leading order $n$ in~\eqref{eq:phaseode} is estimated numerically, and
the natural place to do so is not the imaginary axis at all. The term $n/2$
accounts for the phase gathered on the arc at infinity, and that arc lies in
the \emph{right half-plane}, where every delayed factor $\ee^{-\lam\tau_j}$
decays exponentially. Probing along the positive real axis therefore
measures precisely the quantity the counting formula needs, with the delay
terms already extinguished:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:npow}
  n \;\approx\; \operatorname*{LS\text{-}slope}_{\;s_k\in[s_\ast/10,\;s_\ast]}
  \Bigl(\ln s_k \,\mapsto\, \ln\bigl|\Dfun(s_k)\bigr|\Bigr),
  \qquad s_\ast \gg 1 ,
\end{equation}
the least-squares slope of the log--log magnitude over a handful of
log-spaced real samples. The probe is decoupled from the integration range
($n$ is a property of $\Dfun$ alone; the default is $s_\ast=10^{8}$ whatever
$\wmax$ is), costs a handful of evaluations once per sweep, and converges as
$O(1/s_\ast)$ -- measured here as $7.5\cdot10^{-5}$, $7.4\cdot10^{-7}$ and
$7.4\cdot10^{-9}$ at $s_\ast=10^{4}$, $10^{6}$ and $10^{8}$ -- including the
effective integer order of neutral equations and the non-integer order of
fractional ones. On the imaginary axis, by contrast, the delay terms
superimpose a ripple on $\ln|\Dfun(\ii\omega)|$ that never decays for a
neutral system (a single-point estimator gives $n_{\mathrm{est}}=80\,028$ at
$\omega=10^{6}$ for a true effective order of $2$). Two limitations remain.
$|\Dfun(s)|\sim s^{n}$ overflows for large $n$, so $s_\ast$ is reduced
automatically; the better answer is structural: where the model offers an
open-loop/feedback split, the \emph{return difference}
$\Dfun=\det(\mat I+\mat Q_0^{-1}\mat F)=1+L(\lam)$ has the same
right-half-plane zeros, adds poles only at the (stable) open-loop roots, and
tends to $1$ at infinity, so $n=0$ \emph{exactly} (case study A.9). And
Eq.~\eqref{eq:npow} presumes polynomial growth, which fails for continua
such as $\cosh\bigl(\sqrt{\lam^2+c\lam}\,L\bigr)\sim\ee^{\lam L}$; the
package detects this (the slope is not reproduced one window lower) and fits
along the imaginary axis instead ($n\approx0$ for the rod of
\ref{app:gallery}).

The requested relative and absolute solver tolerances control the local
error of the accumulated phase, measured in radians. Since the exact
winding number is an integer multiple of $\pi$ in the phase, the error of
$\Zraw$ inherits the tolerance directly as a \emph{dimensionless} quantity:
the same default ($10^{-5}$) is appropriate for a machining model and for a
dense $50\times50$ matrix determinant alike, with no problem-dependent
scaling (Section~\ref{sec:integerness}).

\subsection{Rightmost-root estimation as a by-product}\label{sec:tracking}

The peaks of the integrand are not only a numerical nuisance -- they carry
the information where the dominant characteristic roots are. Along the
integration line, $|\Dfun(\sigma+\ii\omega)|^{2}$ dips to a sharp local
minimum at $\omega\approx\omega_r$ for every nearby root
$\lam_r=\sigma_r+\ii\omega_r$, and the smallest minimum belongs to the root
closest to the line, which for $\sigma=0$ is typically the rightmost root
that decides stability. The solver therefore tracks
$\min_\omega|\Dfun|^{2}$ during the march at negligible cost: at every
integrand evaluation the already computed quantities $\Dfun$ and $\Dfun'$
yield the real part of one Newton step towards the root,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigmaest}
  \sest \;=\; \sigma + \Real\!\left(-\frac{\Dfun}{\dd\Dfun/\dd\lam}\right)
        \;=\; \sigma - \frac{\Real\Dfun\cdot\partial_\omega\Imag\Dfun
              - \Imag\Dfun\cdot\partial_\omega\Real\Dfun}{|\Dfun'|^{2}},
\end{equation}
using $\dd\Dfun/\dd\lam=-\ii\,\Dfun'$, and the frequency of the minimum is
stored as $\west$. The pair $(\sest,\west)$ is a first-order estimate of the
dominant root location returned together with $\Zint$. Its accuracy is
highest near the stability boundary, where the root lies close to the line,
provided a sample lands close to the minimum: a Newton step from a sample at a
distance $\delta\omega$ from $\omega_r$ carries an error of order
$|\Dfun_{\lam\lam}/\Dfun_\lam|\,\delta\omega^{2}$, so once the peak is far
narrower than the local step, the estimate is limited by the step rather than
by the distance of the root from the line (Table~\ref{tab:boundarystress}).
Away from the boundary the estimate degrades gracefully, but there only its
sign and magnitude matter -- as a smooth measure of the distance to
instability.

Several roots can be tracked simultaneously. A compile-time variant of the
solver detects every local minimum of $|\Dfun|^{2}$ through the sign change
of its $\omega$-derivative, $2(\Real\Dfun\,\partial_\omega\Real\Dfun +
\Imag\Dfun\,\partial_\omega\Imag\Dfun)$, and maintains the $N_r$ best
candidates in a sorted, allocation-free buffer; a boundary test at
$\omega=0$ captures real dominant roots, since $|\Dfun|^{2}$ is even in
$\omega$. Tracking one root is the default and adds a few percent to the sweep
time; tracking zero roots gives maximal sweep speed. One caveat deserves emphasis: the
\emph{deepest} minimum of $|\Dfun|$ belongs to the rightmost root near the
stability boundary, but deeper inside the stable or unstable domains a
different root branch may produce a deeper dip. When the true dominant root
is required away from the boundary, several minima should be tracked
($N_r\approx5$--$10$), all of them refined, and the dominant root taken as
the one with the maximal real part -- this recipe is validated against an
independent eigenvalue method in Section~\ref{sec:performance-sd}.

The recommendation matters because the single-minimum estimate is not only
inaccurate away from the boundary but \emph{discontinuous}: where one branch
overtakes another as the closest one, $\sest$ jumps, and a tracer that fits a
local linear model (Section~\ref{sec:mdbm}) reads the jump as a spurious zero
crossing. The dominant root, a maximum over branches, passes through such
crossings continuously, so the coloring and the boundary objective are built
from it.

\subsection{Optional higher-order root refinement}\label{sec:refinement}

The raw tracked estimate $(\sest,\west)$ -- one Newton step off the minimum
of $|\Dfun|$ -- is the method's \emph{default} root output, and deliberately
so. It is first-order accurate, but it is the \emph{smoothest} field over a
parameter chart (a single formula evaluated at every pixel), it is the
cheapest, and near the stability boundary, where the classification is
decided, its sign is that of the rightmost root (away from it, the closest
minimum may belong to a non-dominant branch, Section~\ref{sec:tracking}).
When an accurate rightmost root is wanted, the same automatic
derivatives drive an optional local polish. Writing the Taylor expansion at
$\hat\lam=\sest+\ii\west$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:taylor}
  \Dfun(\hat\lam+\Delta) \approx \Dfun + \Dfun_\lam\,\Delta
   + \tfrac12 \Dfun_{\lam\lam}\,\Delta^{2}
   + \tfrac16 \Dfun_{\lam\lam\lam}\,\Delta^{3} = 0,
\end{equation}
where the subscripts denote derivatives with respect to $\lam$ evaluated at
$\hat\lam$, the package offers (i) \emph{Newton--Raphson} iterations
$\Delta=-\Dfun/\Dfun_\lam$; (ii) a single \emph{quadratic} (Poly-2) or (iii)
\emph{cubic} (Poly-3) Taylor solve, where the root of the local polynomial
closest to $\hat\lam$ is selected via the eigenvalues of its companion matrix;
and (iv) a \emph{combined} mode that runs the polynomial and Newton polishes
and keeps, per root, the candidate with the smallest $|\Dfun|$. The first
derivative is exact (automatic differentiation); the higher derivatives are
central differences of that exact first derivative, which avoids the
compilation cost of nested dual numbers. Each polish is \emph{monotone}: it is
accepted only if it lowers $|\Dfun|$ below the seed, otherwise the raw estimate
is returned, so refinement never increases $|\Dfun|$ -- although, as discussed
next, a lower $|\Dfun|$ can belong to a neighboring root.

Which polish is best is system-dependent: on a smooth quasi-polynomial Newton
reaches machine precision, but on a characteristic function with poles a
Newton step can settle on a neighboring root, where the Taylor polishes stay
put; the combined mode is the robust choice at about $\RefCombOver\,\%$ over
the raw solve (Section~\ref{sec:showcase-roots}). Refinement is therefore off
for charts and on when a specific rightmost root matters; a precise
decay-rate \emph{map} comes from the $\sigma$-contours of
Section~\ref{sec:sigmacontours}. Applied to several tracked estimates, the
refinement returns a set of dominant roots, as a dedicated root finder such as
QPmR~\cite{vyhlidal2009mapping} does, here as an optional post-processing
step of the count.

\subsection{Self-validating error estimate}\label{sec:integerness}

The exact winding number is an integer, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eps}
  \varepsilon = \bigl|\Zraw - \operatorname{round}(\Zraw)\bigr|
\end{equation}
is a freely available a-posteriori indicator of the accumulated error -- the
local integration errors, the tail truncated at $\wmax$ and the residual of
the estimated order~\eqref{eq:npow} -- and a necessary condition, not a
certificate. (A root \emph{on} the line has no count; the package returns an
invalid-count marker.) Since the phase is integrated in radians, $\varepsilon$
is dimensionless and scales with the requested tolerance, amplified by the
accepted-step count: the largest residual of a chart follows
$\max\varepsilon\approx100\,\mathrm{tol}$ (Section~\ref{sec:showcase-tolerance}),
so the tolerance is a universal accuracy dial that transfers between systems
without re-tuning; the package default $10^{-5}$ keeps it two orders of
magnitude below the rounding threshold. Whenever the leading order $n$ is
known by inspection, it should simply be passed (\code{n\_power\_max}); the
estimator~\eqref{eq:npow} only spares the user who does not know it.

The diagnostic presumes a \emph{converging tail}. For retarded systems it
does: the median residual of the phase ODE on the retarded case studies of
\ref{app:gallery} is $\sim10^{-4}$ at the chart tolerance $10^{-4}$. For
neutral systems the tail oscillates within $\arcsin(\beta)/\pi$
(Section~\ref{sec:counting}) and the residual inherits it ($0.07$--$0.15$ on
the neutral panels, with correct counts); there the analytic bound
$\arcsin(\beta)/\pi<1/2$ protects the count, not $\varepsilon$.

One blind spot is inherited from the argument principle itself. A march that
steps \emph{over} a peak loses exactly $\pm\pi$ of phase, so $\Zraw$ shifts by
exactly one and remains an integer: $\varepsilon$ measures the quadrature and
cannot see a skipped peak. Near a stability boundary the peak half-width
$|\sigma_r-\sigma|$ does collapse below any practical step
(Table~\ref{tab:boundarystress}), and no integration back-end is immune; the
discrete march of Section~\ref{sec:unwrap}, which samples $\Dfun$ itself,
rejects such steps by construction (Table~\ref{tab:unwrapstress}). Two free
tests detect a skipped peak. The first compares the count with the tracked
root of Section~\ref{sec:tracking}, which shares the samples but not the
accumulation; restricted to the neighborhood of the line -- far from it the
tracked minimum need not be dominant, and a blind test fires on
\CheckNaiveRef{} of the \CheckNpoints{} points of a fully converged chart --
the test
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crosscheck}
  \operatorname{sign}(\sest-\sigma)\ \text{disagrees with}\ [\Zint=0]
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad |\sest-\sigma| < h
\end{equation}
($h$ a small fraction of the spread of $\sest$, here $h=\CheckH$) fires on
only \CheckNearRef{} of them, and it is sharp exactly where a skip flips the
stable/unstable classification. The second is parity: complex roots right of
the line come in conjugate pairs, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parity}
  \Zint \equiv \bigl[\operatorname{sign}\Dfun(\sigma)\neq\operatorname{sign}c\bigr]
  \pmod 2 ,
\end{equation}
with $\Dfun(\sigma)$ and the sign of the leading coefficient $c$ known from
the first sample and the real-axis probe~\eqref{eq:npow}. A single skipped
peak violates~\eqref{eq:parity}; in a repeated boundary stress test it caught
all $13$ miscounts of the ODE march and fired on none of the correct counts,
whereas the sign test~\eqref{eq:crosscheck} missed $5$ of them
(\code{data/unwrap\_checks}). Neither test rescues a chart run far outside the
rule of thumb (at $\mathrm{tol}=\CheckLooseTol$ the near-boundary test catches
only \CheckLooseCaught{} of \CheckLooseFlips{} flips): that failure is
announced by the residual field lighting up over the whole chart. The
minimum of $|\Dfun|^{2}$ also \emph{locates} a skipped peak however narrow it
is, which an optional repair callback uses (\ref{app:backends}).

\subsection{Alternative integrators within the same framework}\label{sec:alternatives}

The phase integrand~\eqref{eq:integrand} can also be fed to adaptive
Gauss--Kronrod quadrature (\pkg{QuadGK.jl}~\cite{johnson2013quadgk}) through
the identical interface; for the count alone it is competitive, within a
factor of about three in evaluations either way (Table~\ref{tab:evals}).
It visits the frequencies out of order, however, so no local minimum of
$|\Dfun|^{2}$ can be recognized on the fly -- and every chart in this paper
is built from the tracked root: the coloring~\eqref{eq:coloring}, the
boundary objective~\eqref{eq:objective}, the refinement seeds and the
cross-check~\eqref{eq:crosscheck}. This ordered traversal, not accuracy or
stiffness, is why the phase ODE is the default integration back-end. A
fixed-step trapezoidal back-end is included as a real-time floor, but a
uniform grid cannot resolve the peak~\eqref{eq:peak} of a root close to the
line: its mean count error converges, its worst error over a chart does not
(\ref{app:backends}). The fourth back-end keeps the adaptivity and the
ordered traversal but drops the integration itself.
\subsection{Discrete phase unwrapping: counting without integration}\label{sec:unwrap}

The phase ODE~\eqref{eq:phaseode} integrates $\theta'$ only to obtain one
number, the end value $y(\wmax)=\theta(\wmax)-\theta(0)$ of the continuous
phase $\theta(\omega)=\arg\Dfun(\sigma+\ii\omega)$. That number is available
\emph{without} integration. Between two samples $\omega_a<\omega_b$ the phase
increment is known exactly up to a multiple of $2\pi$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dphase}
  \Delta_{ab} = \operatorname{Arg}\frac{\Dfun(\sigma+\ii\omega_b)}{\Dfun(\sigma+\ii\omega_a)}
  \in(-\pi,\pi],
\end{equation}
and it equals the true increment $\Phi(\omega_b)-\Phi(\omega_a)$ whenever
the latter lies in $(-\pi,\pi)$. On any sampling
$0=\omega_0<\omega_1<\dots<\omega_K=\wmax$ that is dense enough in this sense,
the counting formula~\eqref{eq:count} becomes the finite sum
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unwrapcount}
  \Zraw = \frac{n}{2} - \frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Delta_{k-1,k},
\end{equation}
which is \emph{exact}: there is no integration error, only the requirement
that no branch of the phase is lost -- the classical phase-unwrapping problem
(Tribolet~\cite{tribolet1977new}; Ying and Katz~\cite{ying1988reliable}, with
a rigorous variant in~\cite{johnson2009enclosing}; for delay systems
St\'ep\'an~\cite{stepan1989retarded} and Hassard~\cite{hassard1997counting};
cf.\ Section~\ref{sec:intro} for what is new here). The same dual-number
evaluation that returns $\Dfun$ also returns the exact phase slope
$\theta'=\Imag(\Dfun'/\Dfun)$ at every sample.

\paragraph{Adaptivity.} The slope makes the march adaptive in exactly the way
an embedded Runge--Kutta pair is. A trial step $h=\omega_b-\omega_a$ evaluates
$\Dfun$ and $\Dfun'$ at $\omega_b$ and compares the exact (observed) increment
with the trapezoidal value predicted by the exact slopes at both ends,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uwerr}
  e = \Bigl|\,\Delta_{ab} - \tfrac{h}{2}\bigl(\theta'_a+\theta'_b\bigr)\Bigr| ,
\end{equation}
accepts the step if $e\le\mathrm{tol}$ (in radians; default $0.3$), and sets
the next trial step to $h\cdot\min\{4,\max\{0.2,\,0.9(\mathrm{tol}/e)^{1/3}\}\}$,
the exponent reflecting the $O(h^{3}\theta''')$ trapezoid error -- the standard
step-size controller, with the exact increment in the role of the
higher-order solution. The acceptance test is Tribolet's consistency check;
the controller, which also lets the step grow, is what crosses the
semi-infinite frequency axis in few steps. Unlike the solver tolerance of
Section~\ref{sec:phaseode}, this tolerance does not set the error of $\Zraw$
-- the sum~\eqref{eq:unwrapcount} has none -- but is a safety margin against
losing a branch, and it is far from critical (\ref{app:unwrap}). On
smooth stretches $e$ follows the curvature of the phase. Across an unresolved root transition -- a peak~\eqref{eq:peak} of width
$|\sigma_r-\sigma|$ hidden inside the step -- the observed increment contains a
jump of almost $\pm\pi$ that the end-point slopes do not predict, $e\approx\pi$,
and the step is rejected and refined until the transition is resolved. In the
tail of a retarded system, where $\theta$ approaches its limit up to a
decaying ripple, $e\to0$ and the step grows geometrically, so the frequency
axis is crossed in a logarithmic number of steps whatever $\wmax$ is. (For a
neutral system the ripple does not decay, the step stays near
$\mathrm{tol}/(\beta\tau)$ and the cost grows linearly with $\wmax$, so a
neutral chart needs an explicit, moderate $\wmax$: with the windows of
\ref{app:gallery}, $200$--$500$, the march reproduces every defined count of
the three neutral panels at a median of $260$--$650$ evaluations per point.)
The delay-induced ripple that keeps an integrator busy for thousands of steps
(Section~\ref{sec:phaseode}) becomes invisible once its phase amplitude drops
below the tolerance, because its contribution is contained \emph{exactly} in
the end-point phase. Each step costs a single
evaluation of $\Dfun$ (a \code{Vern9} step costs sixteen), and on the systems of
this paper a median point needs $30$--$130$ evaluations instead of
$10^{3}$--$10^{4}$ (Section~\ref{sec:performance-unwrap}). Automatic
differentiation is still essential: the error estimate~\eqref{eq:uwerr} needs the
exact slope, and the root tracking below needs $\Dfun'$.

\paragraph{Root tracking without extra evaluations.} The rightmost-root
by-product of Section~\ref{sec:tracking} carries over. A minimum of $|\Dfun|$
inside an accepted step is recognized by the sign change of
$\partial_\omega|\Dfun|$ between its end points; it is located on the cubic
Hermite interpolant of the complex function $\Dfun(\omega)$ built from $\Dfun$
and $\Dfun'$ at both ends -- $\Dfun$ itself is smooth even where its phase is
not -- and one Newton step~\eqref{eq:sigmaest} from the located minimum gives
$(\sest,\west)$; the estimate is kept only if the Newton step stays within
the step that contains the minimum. No additional evaluation of $\Dfun$ is
spent. The minimum is detected through the sign change of
$\partial_\omega|\Dfun|$ between the end points, so a step that contains a
minimum and a neighboring maximum of $|\Dfun|$ hides the minimum; for a
dominant root near the line the step is small and this does not happen.

\paragraph{Below the resolution of the number format.} When a root lies so
close to the line that its transition is narrower than a floor of a few
machine epsilons of the frequency ($h\le8\,\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\max(\omega,1)$
in the format in which the march runs), the step is not refined further and
the observed increment is ambiguous near $\pm\pi$.
The branch is then decided by the \emph{side} of the root: a root right of the
line lowers the phase by $\pi$, one left of it raises it, and the side is the
sign of the Newton estimate from the current sample -- the peak repair of
Section~\ref{sec:showcase-tolerance} at no cost. Such a count is a decision
rather than a measurement, and the point is flagged. Since the sum
\eqref{eq:unwrapcount} carries no integration error, the residual
$\varepsilon$ of~\eqref{eq:eps} now contains only the truncated tail and the
error of the order $n$: for retarded systems it is typically
$10^{-7}$--$10^{-10}$, and a value above $1/4$ flags a real root \emph{on}
the line ($\Dfun(\sigma)=0$, $\Zraw=k+\tfrac12$). A complex pair exactly on
the line is not flagged -- the count is then undefined, and the march returns
one of the adjacent values -- and for a neutral system the residual carries
the bounded oscillation of Section~\ref{sec:counting}, which exceeds $1/4$
for a neutral amplitude above about $0.7$, so that the flag is not usable for
that class.

\paragraph{Limitations.} An end-point test cannot see an increment of exactly
$2\pi$: two transitions in the same direction inside one step escape it. Two
situations produced them on the benchmarks. First, a $\Dfun$ with poles -- a
return difference $1+L(\lam)$ -- places a pole next to every lightly damped
mode; when a root leaves that mode into the right half-plane, the pole--zero
pair winds the phase by $-2\pi$ within a frequency interval of the size of the
modal damping. Writing $\Dfun$ as an entire function -- multiplying out the
(stable) denominators, which does not change the count in
$\Real\lam>\sigma$ -- removes the pair. This reverses the advice of
Section~\ref{sec:phaseode} for the return difference, whose $n=0$ and moderate
magnitudes favor the phase ODE; for large models with a low-rank feedback a
step cap near the known open-loop poles would keep both advantages. Second, a
regenerative delay $\tau$ can place a chain of roots near the axis, spaced
$\approx2\pi/\tau$ apart in frequency; capping the step at
$h\le\pi/(2\tau_{\max})$ over the resonance band removes the ambiguity at a
moderate cost (for the turning model of Table~\ref{tab:unwrap},
$h\le0.05$ for $\omega<5$). A third situation is generic though it did not
occur on the benchmarks: two lightly damped roots right of the line within
one step, as near a double-Hopf point of a multi-mode system; the band cap
covers it only if it is below their frequency separation. The step test is
therefore a strong safeguard rather than a proof: on the $50\times50$
determinant of \ref{app:gallery} (A.10), with fifty lightly damped
modes packed into one decade of frequency, no setting removed all miscounts.
With these rules the discrete march reproduces the reference counts on every
point of the three benchmark charts and of nine gallery panels and the
showcase (\ref{app:gallery}), apart from points with a root exactly
on the line, and near a Hopf boundary it is more robust than the phase ODE
(Table~\ref{tab:unwrapstress}). Where a parameter-independent, stable
denominator cannot be cleared conveniently -- $\cosh\gamma$ of a continuum,
$\det\mat Q_0$ of a finite element model -- its phase can be measured once
along the line and used as an effective order,
$n_{\mathrm{eff}}=2\Phi_{\mathrm{den}}(\wmax)/\pi$, while the march counts
the entire numerator (A.8, A.9).

\paragraph{Parallel hardware.} The march is a loop of identical scalar steps
on a fixed-size state, one evaluation each, with no allocation; its execution
on CPUs and GPUs is analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:parallel}, and the algorithm
and its parameters are listed in \ref{app:unwrap}.
