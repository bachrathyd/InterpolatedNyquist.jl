\section{Case-study gallery}\label{app:gallery}

This appendix demonstrates the identical workflow of
Section~\ref{sec:workflow} -- coarse colored sweep, MDBM boundary, combined
coloring~\eqref{eq:coloring} -- on eleven further systems covering all
supported model classes, plus the reproduction of a published design
chart. The complete scripts, grid resolutions and timings are part of the
package repository.

\paragraph{These charts are the speed argument.} Sections~\ref{sec:showcase}
and~\ref{sec:performance} make the accuracy case, at $\wmax=10^{6}$ and
tolerances down to $10^{-8}$. The gallery answers the complementary question
-- \emph{what does a chart cost when one simply wants to see the picture?} --
and so every panel here is computed at settings chosen for speed rather than
for certainty: a generous $100\times100$ background grid, but $\wmax$ only a
few decades above the highest resonance and a tolerance of $10^{-4}$, one
decade looser than the package default. Each panel is annotated with the
measured wall-clock cost of both stages. At these settings the count of a few
boundary-adjacent pixels may be off by one (Section~\ref{sec:showcase-tolerance});
that is precisely the trade a first exploration should make, it is invisible
at chart resolution, and the traced boundary -- which is built from $\sest$,
not from the count -- is unaffected. The MDBM traces start from a $7\times7$
mesh and refine six times, reaching a $448\times448$-equivalent boundary
resolution -- roughly four times the background grid -- from $49$ blanket
evaluations.

\paragraph{Reading the charts.} Every panel uses the same convention, the
combined field $C$ of~\eqref{eq:coloring}: $\sest$ where $\Zint=0$, and
$\Zint$ elsewhere. Outside
the stable domain the colour encodes the \emph{integer} number of
characteristic roots to the right of the imaginary axis, so those regions
appear as flat plateaus with sharp steps between them. Inside the stable
domain ($\Zint=0$) the same colour axis continues \emph{below zero} and
encodes the estimated decay rate $\sest<0$, i.e.\ the spectral gap: the
darker the shade, the faster every disturbance decays, and the colour varies
smoothly because $\sest$ does. One map therefore answers three questions at
once -- where the system is stable, how robustly, and how many roots must be
stabilized elsewhere. Following Section~\ref{sec:tracking}, the shading uses
the \emph{dominant} root -- five tracked minima, all refined, maximal real
part -- rather than the single closest minimum, which would switch branch
where another mode overtakes it and shade the deep stable domain with a
non-dominant root. The black curve is the stability boundary traced by
MDBM from the objective~\eqref{eq:objective}, not a contour of the coloured
grid; the same dominant-root convention makes that objective continuous
across a branch crossing, which matters more than it sounds -- a jump there
lets MDBM's linear in-cell interpolation invent a spurious boundary point in
the middle of the stable domain. Each panel is annotated with the wall-clock
cost of both stages: the brute-force sweep that produces the colour, and the
MDBM trace that produces the boundary.

\paragraph{A.1 Fourth-order delayed oscillator.}
$\Dfun(\lam)=c_1\lam^4+\lam^2+2\zeta\lam+1+(P+D\lam)\ee^{-\lam\tau}$ with
$c_1=0.03$, $\zeta=0.02$, $\tau=0.5$, swept over the control gains
$(P,D)$. This is the regression benchmark of the package (test suite and
performance history) and the system of the solver comparison in
Fig.~\ref{fig:zoo}.

\paragraph{A.2 Delayed oscillator.}
$\Dfun(\lam)=\lam^2+a\lam+b\,\ee^{-\lam/2}$ over $(a,b)$: the textbook
retarded case with both divergence and Hopf boundaries.

\paragraph{A.3 Distributed delay.}
$\Dfun(\lam)=\lam^2+a\lam+b\,(1-\ee^{-\lam\tau})/\lam$, $\tau=1$, i.e., a
uniform delay kernel $\int_0^\tau x(t-\vartheta)\dd\vartheta$. The kernel
integral appears in closed form in $\Dfun$; the removable singularity at
$\lam=0$ is handled by the series fallback in the model code and by the
origin offset of Section~\ref{sec:phaseode}.

\paragraph{A.4--A.5 Neutral systems.}
$\Dfun(\lam)=\lam^2+a\lam^2\ee^{-\lam}+1+c\,\ee^{-\lam}$ and its high-gain
damped variant $\Dfun(\lam)=\lam^2+a\lam^2\ee^{-\lam}+5\lam+c\,\ee^{-\lam}$,
swept over $(a,c)$ with $|a|<1$. The delayed acceleration term makes the
equations neutral: the phase integrand oscillates persistently up to
arbitrarily high frequencies, so the truncation is kept moderate
($\wmax=200$) -- the resulting tail error is bounded by
$\arcsin|a|/\pi<1/2$ and never corrupts the rounded count -- and the
leading-order fit~\eqref{eq:npow} delivers the effective order $n=2$ where
the naive point estimator diverges.

\paragraph{A.6 PDA control and essential instability.}
Acceleration feedback is popular in active vibration control, but the
delayed acceleration term $A\ddot x(t-\tau)$ turns the closed loop into a
neutral system with characteristic function
\begin{equation*}
  \Dfun(\lam)=\lam^2+2\zeta\lam+1+(P+D\lam+A\lam^2)\,\ee^{-\lam\tau},
\end{equation*}
whose neutral coefficient is $1+A\ee^{-\lam\tau}$. For $|A|>1$ the
essential spectrum moves to $\Real\lam=\ln|A|/\tau>0$: infinitely many
roots become unstable and $\Zint\to\infty$. With a finite $\wmax$ the
computed count saturates at a large value ($\sim\wmax\tau/(2\pi)$), so the
method cannot -- and need not -- report the exact count there; crucially,
the \emph{classification} into stable and unstable domains remains correct,
and the chart in Fig.~\ref{fig:gallerya} displays both the regular
Hopf-type lobes and the analytic essential boundaries $|A|=1$, which the
MDBM trace locates without special treatment. The count is capped in the
plot for readability. This case study substantiates the claim of
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} that essential loss of stability is detected
robustly even though the count itself diverges.

\paragraph{A.7 Multi-mode turning lobes.}
The regenerative turning model with a two-mode flexible structure,
$\Dfun(\lam)=1+w\,(1-\ee^{-2\pi\lam/\Omega})\sum_{k=1}^{2}
g_k/(\lam^2+2\zeta_k\omega_k\lam+\omega_k^2)$, over spindle speed $\Omega$
and chip width $w$~\cite{tobias1958theory,altintas1995analytical,stepan2001modelling}.
Two features deserve emphasis. First, $\Dfun$ is \emph{rational} -- its
structural poles lie in the open left half-plane, so the argument-principle
count is unaffected, and $\Dfun\to1$ at high frequency gives an effective
order $n\approx0$; no polynomial form is ever constructed. Second, the
chart makes the introductory argument visible: towards low spindle speeds
the lobes accumulate, and a D-curve-based method would trace an unbounded
family of curves of which almost none bounds the stable region, whereas the
boundary objective~\eqref{eq:objective} follows only the true stability
limit~\cite{bachrathy2013improved}.

\paragraph{A.8 Transcendental beam, and why the damping model decides
everything.}
Longitudinal vibration of an elastic bar with delayed boundary feedback gives
-- \emph{without any spatial discretization} -- the exact transcendental
return difference
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beam}
  \Dfun(\lam) = 1 + K_p\,\ee^{-\lam\tau}\,\operatorname{sech}\bigl(\gamma(\lam)\,L\bigr),
  \qquad L=1,
\end{equation}
cf.\ the exact analysis of Zhang and St\'ep\'an~\cite{zhang2016exact} and the
finite-DOF hierarchy of Kidd and St\'ep\'an~\cite{kidd2014delayed}. Whether a
finite count even exists is decided entirely by the damping model, and this is
the instructive point of the example. With external (viscous) damping,
$\gamma=\sqrt{\lam^2+c\lam}$, every mode is damped equally: the closed-loop
roots accumulate along a vertical line $\Real\lam\to\mathrm{const}$, so the
number of unstable roots is $0$ or $\infty$ -- the essential-spectrum
obstruction of a neutral system (A.6) -- and $\operatorname{sech}(\gamma L)$
does not decay on the imaginary axis. Real materials, however, dissipate
through \emph{rate-dependent} (Kelvin--Voigt) internal damping,
$\gamma=\lam/\sqrt{1+c\lam}$, for which $\gamma\sim\sqrt{\lam/c}$: the high
modes are damped ever harder, $\Real\lam_k\to-\infty$, only finitely many
roots sit near the axis, and $|\operatorname{sech}(\gamma L)|$ decays like
$\ee^{-\sqrt{\omega/2c}}$. Note how little is needed: the chart uses
$c=0.02$, a light damping that leaves the low modes essentially unaffected
and the chart shape intact, yet it is what makes the count exist at all.
The chart over $(K_p,\tau)$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:galleryb} shows the resulting
stable bands (the physically wrong viscous model gives a nonsensical count
for the same gains -- a diagnostic that the model, not the method, is at
fault). The example thus doubles as a reminder that the right damping law is a
modelling decision the method faithfully reflects rather than papers over.

\paragraph{A.9 Finite element bar (29 DOF), and the return difference.}
The same bar discretized by 29 linear finite elements, with stiffness-
proportional (Kelvin--Voigt) damping $\mat C=c\mat K$. The naive formulation
is the raw determinant $\det(\lam^2\mat M+\lam\mat C+\mat K + K_p\,h^{-1}
\ee^{-\lam\tau}\,\vect e_{29}\vect e_1^{\!\top})$ ($h$ = element length), and
it is a poor one: it grows like $\lam^{58}$, reaches magnitudes of
$\sim\FemRawMag$ before overflowing, and forces the counting formula onto an
estimate of a very large leading order -- which leaves an integer residual of
$\FemRawResid$, a rounded count barely better than a coin toss. Factoring out
the open-loop dynamics,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:returndiff}
  \Dfun(\lam) = \det\!\bigl(\mat I + \mat Q_0(\lam)^{-1}\mat F(\lam)\bigr),
  \qquad \mat Q_0 = \lam^2\mat M+\lam\mat C+\mat K,
\end{equation}
where $\mat F(\lam)=K_p h^{-1}\ee^{-\lam\tau}\vect e_{29}\vect e_1^{\!\top}$
is the (rank-one) feedback matrix, leaves the zeros untouched, adds poles
only at the open-loop roots -- all in the left half-plane, so the
right-half-plane count is unaffected -- and yields $\Dfun\to1$ at infinity,
hence $n=0$ exactly and a residual of $\FemRdResid$. Because the feedback
here is a single collocated sensor--actuator pair, $\mat F$ is rank one, and
the matrix determinant lemma collapses~\eqref{eq:returndiff} to the classical
scalar return difference $1+L(\lam)$ of~\eqref{eq:beam},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:detlemma}
  \Dfun(\lam) = 1 + K_p h^{-1}\ee^{-\lam\tau}\,
                \vect e_1^{\!\top}\mat Q_0(\lam)^{-1}\vect e_{29},
\end{equation}
which replaces a full $29\times29$ solve \emph{and} a $29\times29$
determinant by one solve with a single right-hand side: agreeing with the
determinant form to $\FemLemmaAgree$ relative and $\FemLemmaSpeedup$ times
faster per evaluation, which is what makes this panel affordable at the
gallery's resolution. For multi-input feedback of
rank $r$ the same lemma leaves an $r\times r$ determinant. This is the
large-system economy that Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} points to, and it is
worth more than any tuning of the integrator. One caveat is inherited from classical loop analysis: an exact
pole--zero cancellation between the open loop and the feedback (an
unobservable or uncontrollable mode) would remove that root
from~\eqref{eq:returndiff} -- as it does from every input--output method --
so the split should only be applied when the open-loop model is stabilizable
and detectable through the chosen loop. The finite element chart reproduces
the continuum result of A.8 within the resolved frequency range: the two are
consistency checks on each other. As Section~\ref{sec:phaseode} argues,
where such an open-loop/feedback split exists it is worth more than any
tuning of the integrator.

\paragraph{A.10 Large dense determinant.}
A synthetic stress test: $\Dfun(\lam)=\det(\lam^2\mat M+\lam\mat C+\mat K+
g\,\ee^{-\lam\tau}\mat K)$ with dense random symmetric positive definite
$50\times50$ matrices, $\mat C=0.25\,\mat K$, swept over a delayed stiffness
gain $g>-1$ and the delay $\tau$. (At $g=-1$ exactly, the delayed stiffness
cancels the static one and a characteristic root sits \emph{on} the
integration line; the package then reports the invalid-count marker instead
of an arbitrary integer.) The evaluation cost is dominated by the LU
factorization per frequency; the adaptive sweep keeps the number of
evaluations low, so even this case yields charts in tens of seconds. This
example also exercises the overflow guards of
Sections~\ref{sec:phaseode}--\ref{sec:tracking}: $|\Dfun|$ reaches
$10^{250}$ and beyond, so the phase integrand is evaluated in scale-invariant
form and the leading-order probe~\eqref{eq:npow} automatically retreats to a
representable $s_\ast$.

\paragraph{A.11 Fractional-order oscillator.}
$\Dfun(\lam)=\lam^{1.8}+0.5\,\lam^{0.8}+k\,\ee^{-\lam\tau}$ over
$(k,\tau)$, with the principal branch of the fractional powers. The
half-line count applies with the non-integer effective order estimated
by~\eqref{eq:npow}; the origin offset of Section~\ref{sec:phaseode}
regularizes the branch point at $\lam=0$. For $\sigma=0$ the integration
line stays right of the branch cut and the count is unambiguous; shifted
lines with $\sigma<0$ cross the cut of the principal branch, so
$\gamma$-stability contours of fractional systems refer to the roots of the
principal-branch extension of $\Dfun$ (see Section~\ref{sec:counting}) --
consistent with the convention of the fractional-systems
literature~\cite{petras2011fractional,fioravanti2012numerical}, but worth
keeping in mind when comparing against time-domain simulations.

\paragraph{A.11 Fractional controller chart from the literature.}
As an external validation of the fractional capability,
Fig.~\ref{fig:fraccontroller} reproduces the stabilizing-region chart of
Gao, Zhai and Liu~\cite{gao2017robust} (Example~1), computed there by
D-decomposition; see also the related design
charts~\cite{hamamci2007algorithm,hohenbichler2008comments,ruszewski2008stability,cheng2006stabilization}.
The closed-loop characteristic function
$\Dfun(s)=s^{\mu}(10s^{0.5}+1)+5\ee^{-0.4s}(k_p s^{\mu}+k_i)$ is a
one-line model definition for the present method; the traced boundaries and
the classification of the published test points agree with the reference,
including the shrinking regions of prescribed stability degree obtained
with shifted integration lines.
