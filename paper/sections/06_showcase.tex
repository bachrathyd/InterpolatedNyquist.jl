\section{Showcase: a constrained two-degree-of-freedom system with delayed PD control}\label{sec:showcase}

All elements of the method are now demonstrated on a single mechanical
example that is simple enough to verify by hand yet exercises every
nontrivial feature at once: multiple degrees of freedom, a feedback delay,
and an algebraic constraint, i.e., a delay differential--algebraic system
with a singular mass matrix.

\subsection{Model and automatic extraction}\label{sec:showcase-model}

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \maybefig{fig_showcase_hybrid}{0.72\linewidth}
  \caption{Stability chart of the constrained two-degree-of-freedom system
  with delayed PD control. Colored background: combined
  field~\eqref{eq:coloring} of the brute-force sweep (smooth spectral gap
  $\sest$ inside the stable domain, integer number of unstable roots
  outside). Black curve: stability boundary traced by MDBM with the scalar
  objective~\eqref{eq:objective}. Red ellipse and star: largest inscribed
  ellipse and the most robust parameter point. The markers S and U denote
  the stable and unstable points analyzed in
  Fig.~\ref{fig:walkthrough}.}\label{fig:showcase}
\end{figure}

The main structure of mass $m_1$ is statically unstable
(inverted-pendulum-like, modeled by the negative net suspension stiffness
$k_1<0$ and damping $c_1$); it carries an absorber unit of mass $m_2$
attached through a spring and damper $(k_2, c_2)$, and an instrumentation
block of mass $m_3$ is \emph{rigidly bolted} to the absorber mass. Instead of
eliminating the rigid connection by hand, the model keeps it as an algebraic
constraint with the internal force $F_{\mathrm c}$ -- exactly the form in
which a simulation model of a larger assembly would be written. A digital
controller measures the motion of the main mass and applies the delayed PD
force $u(t) = -P\,x_1(t-\tau) - D\,\dot x_1(t-\tau)$ to it. With the state
vector $\vect z = (x_1,x_2,x_3,v_1,v_2,v_3,F_{\mathrm c})$, the descriptor
form~\eqref{eq:ddae} reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:showcase}
  \underbrace{\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,m_1,m_2,m_3,0)}_{\textstyle\mat E}\,
  \dot{\vect z}(t) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    v_1\\[1pt] v_2\\[1pt] v_3\\[1pt]
    -k_1 x_1 - c_1 v_1 + k_2(x_2-x_1) + c_2(v_2-v_1) + u(t)\\[1pt]
    -k_2(x_2-x_1) - c_2(v_2-v_1) + F_{\mathrm c}\\[1pt]
    -F_{\mathrm c}\\[1pt]
    x_3 - x_2
  \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with the numerical values $m_1=1$, $m_2=0.3$, $m_3=0.2$, $k_1=-1$, $k_2=1$,
$c_1=c_2=0.05$ and $\tau=0.5$. The last row of~\eqref{eq:showcase} carries no
time derivative -- the mass matrix is singular -- and the model is passed to
the extraction of Section~\ref{sec:extraction} exactly as written, in its
simulation (right-hand-side) form. For verification, the constraint is also
eliminated by hand ($x_3=x_2$, merged mass $m_{23}=m_2+m_3$), which yields
the reduced quasi-polynomial
\begin{equation}\label{eq:showcasered}
  \Dfun_{\mathrm{red}}(\lam)=\det\!\begin{pmatrix}
   m_1\lam^2+(c_1{+}c_2)\lam+k_1{+}k_2+(P{+}D\lam)\ee^{-\lam\tau} &
   -(c_2\lam+k_2)\\
   -(c_2\lam+k_2) &
   m_{23}\lam^2+c_2\lam+k_2
  \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The ratio of the automatically extracted $7\times7$ determinant to
$\Dfun_{\mathrm{red}}$ is constant in $\lam$ to machine precision
(deviation below $10^{-15}$ in the computed charts), which validates the
mass-matrix extraction path end to end; the estimated leading
order~\eqref{eq:npow} is $n\approx4.000$, as expected for two mechanical
degrees of freedom. All results below were computed with the automatically
extracted characteristic function.

\subsection{The chart and the phase integration at work}\label{sec:showcase-chart}

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \maybefig{fig_method_walkthrough}{\linewidth}
  \caption{The method at the stable point S and unstable point U of
  Fig.~\ref{fig:showcase}. (a)~Normalized locus of
  $\Dfun(\ii\omega)$: the stable curve avoids the origin, the unstable one
  encircles it. (b)~Phase slope $\theta'(\omega)$ with the accepted steps of
  the adaptive solver (dots): steps cluster automatically at the
  near-singular peaks that indicate close characteristic roots.
  (c)~Accumulated phase up to $\wmax=10^{4}$ on a logarithmic frequency
  axis: past the resonances the solver keeps resolving the slowly decaying
  ($\omega^{-1}$) delay ripple of this velocity-feedback system with
  moderate, growing steps.}\label{fig:walkthrough}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:showcase} shows the resulting stability chart over the
control-gain plane $(P,D)$. Since the uncontrolled structure is unstable,
the stable domain is a bounded island: its left boundary is the static
(divergence) limit, while the remaining boundaries are Hopf-type curves whose
crossing frequencies are delivered by $\west$. The combined
coloring~\eqref{eq:coloring} displays the interior spectral gap -- deep blue
marks the fastest-decaying configurations -- and the integer plateaus
$\Zint=1,2,3,4$ outside, all in one map. The black MDBM curve is the
high-resolution stability boundary traced from the scalar
objective~\eqref{eq:objective}, and the red star marks the most robust
gain combination found by the largest inscribed ellipse.

Figure~\ref{fig:walkthrough} dissects the single-point computation at the
two marked points. At the stable point S the locus of $\Dfun(\ii\omega)$
avoids the origin and the accumulated phase settles at $\Delta\theta=n\pi/2$,
giving $\Zraw\approx0$; at the unstable point U the locus encircles the
origin and $\Zraw\approx2$. Panel~(b) makes the central numerical argument
of this paper visible: the accepted steps of the adaptive solver concentrate
at the sharp peaks of $\theta'$ near the lightly damped resonances --
precisely the features a fixed frequency grid tends to miss. Panel~(c) shows
the same march continuing over more than two further decades of frequency:
since the delayed \emph{velocity} feedback of this system produces a phase
ripple decaying only like $\omega^{-1}$ (Section~\ref{sec:phaseode}), the
solver keeps taking moderate steps through the tail. This section is about
accuracy, so the accuracy-critical studies below use the safe
$\wmax=10^{6}$; $\wmax=10^{4}$ -- three decades above the resonances --
leaves a truncated tail of $\approx0.2/\wmax\approx2\cdot10^{-5}$ in $\Zraw$,
far below the rounding threshold, and is the economical choice for the charts
themselves and for the gallery of Appendix~\ref{app:gallery}. Either way the
truncation is exposed by the integer-residual diagnostic below, never hidden.

Figure~\ref{fig:tolerance} shows the identical chart computed with
tolerances $10^{-2}$, $10^{-4}$, $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-8}$, together with the
measured sweep times, and Fig.~\ref{fig:errorfield} shows the corresponding
integer-residual fields~\eqref{eq:eps}. The ladder deliberately starts \emph{beyond} the safe range: at $10^{-2}$ the
rule of thumb~\eqref{eq:rule} predicts residuals of order one, i.e.\ genuine
miscounts. Three observations matter for practice. First, the tolerance buys
\emph{certainty}, not appearance. The panels are visually near-identical, yet
the loosest settings do miscount: measured against the $10^{-8}$ reference,
the number of deviating grid points falls steeply with the tolerance
(Table~\ref{tab:tolerance}, last two columns), and this is worth spelling out
because it is the one error the diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:integerness}
cannot report. Every deviation is exactly $\pm1$, and the corresponding
integer residual stays far below the rounding threshold $1/2$ -- the affected
points look perfectly converged. They are skipped peaks, and they are the
practical face of the caveat that Section~\ref{sec:integerness} states in
principle: at the loosest setting the residual field of
Fig.~\ref{fig:errorfield} is uniformly healthy while a handful of pixels are
nevertheless wrong. What does report them is the
cross-check~\eqref{eq:crosscheck}: of the miscounts that flip the
stable/unstable classification -- the only ones a stability chart displays --
it catches all, at the price of a few flagged pixels on a converged chart.
Second -- and this is the most useful outcome for a user -- the integer
residual tracks the requested tolerance across the whole parameter plane
with a roughly constant factor, so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rule}
  \varepsilon \;\approx\; 100 \cdot \mathrm{tol}
\end{equation}
holds to within an order of magnitude over six decades
(Table~\ref{tab:tolerance}, last two columns). The factor is not universal
-- it is inherited from the number of accepted steps, and a march that
resolves a slowly decaying ripple accumulates more of them -- but it is
remarkably stable across the chart and across the systems studied here.
Equation~\eqref{eq:rule} therefore lets the accuracy of $\Zint$ be
\emph{prescribed} rather than discovered: a user who wants the count certain
to $10^{-3}$ asks for $10^{-5}$, which is exactly the package default.
Third, the CPU-time premium of tightening the tolerance by six decades is
well below two orders of magnitude, because the extra effort is spent only
near the integrand peaks -- and this asymmetry has a practical corollary
worth stating plainly, since it runs against the instinct that a loose
tolerance is dangerous.

A miscount is not the same kind of error as a displaced boundary, and for
chart work it is the milder one. The two outputs of the sweep degrade
completely differently with the tolerance: the count $\Zint$ is a
\emph{rounded} quantity, so it fails discontinuously -- an accumulated phase
error of $\pm\pi$ flips it by one -- whereas the tracked $\sest$ is a
\emph{local} quantity read off the minimum of $|\Dfun|$, and it inherits the
tolerance smoothly. The traced stability boundary is built from $\sest$
alone through the objective~\eqref{eq:objective}, so it stays accurate even
where the count does not: Table~\ref{tab:tolerance} lists the counts that
flip, while the traced boundary of the same chart stays a small fraction of
one grid pixel away from the reference trace at every tolerance studied
(\code{data/boundary\_shift.csv}). The practical recipe follows:
\emph{a loose tolerance is an appropriate tool for an initial search} --
locating the stable island, its shape, and the robust operating point --
after which the interesting region is recomputed at the default or tighter
setting to certify the counts. What must not be done is to report a count
from a loose sweep without the cross-check~\eqref{eq:crosscheck}, because
that is the one number whose error the residual will not reveal.

Two limits deserve mention, since both are visible in
Fig.~\ref{fig:errorfield} if the settings are pushed. The rule~\eqref{eq:rule}
holds only while the quadrature is the dominant error: below it lie the
truncated tail (of order $0.2/\wmax$ for this velocity-feedback system,
which is why $\wmax=10^{6}$ is used here rather than the $10^{4}$ that
suffices for the chart itself) and the residual of the estimated leading
order~\eqref{eq:npow}, of order $10^{-9}$. Whichever of the three is largest
sets the observed $\varepsilon$ -- and because all three are visible in the
same number, the diagnostic tells the user which dial to turn next.

Figure~\ref{fig:refinement} quantifies the rightmost-root estimates. The
raw tracked value $(\sest,\west)$ -- one Newton step evaluated at the
integrand minimum -- is near-exact on the stability boundary, accurate to
a few percent in its vicinity, and degrades farther away, exactly as
predicted in Section~\ref{sec:tracking}. What the timings settle is that the
choice of refinement is essentially free: measured per parameter point
against the complete solve, every variant adds only a few percent, because
the refinement costs a handful of extra evaluations of $\Dfun$ against the
thousands the frequency march already spent. Four Newton iterations are in
fact \emph{cheaper} than a cubic Taylor solve -- the latter needs finite
differences and a companion-matrix eigenvalue problem -- and are
simultaneously the most accurate, reaching machine precision over almost the
whole plane (Table~\ref{tab:refinement}). Since it is both better and
cheaper, Newton-4 is the package default; the Taylor variants remain
available and are the more conservative choice when a seed is poor, because
they select the nearest root of a local polynomial rather than following the
Newton flow. For stability charts the hierarchy is in any case exactly
right: on the boundary, where the root position defines the chart, the
estimates are near-exact; deep inside the domains, where only the sign
matters, even the raw estimate suffices. Tracking $N_r>1$ minima returns
several dominant roots at once, which is what the multi-root recipe of
Section~\ref{sec:performance-sd} exploits.
